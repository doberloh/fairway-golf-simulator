# Fairway — a world of golf

A portable 3D browser golf simulator. Open **dist/index.html** directly in a modern browser. The single file is about 15.8 MB (6.4 MB gzipped) and contains the renderer, controls, assets and physics; most of that is the generated redwood forest, which is shipped whole rather than at reduced detail because measurement showed the geometry costs nothing on this path. ordinary play needs no installation, account, or internet connection. WebGL 2 and hardware acceleration are required.

For device-specific setup, source builds, mobile access and troubleshooting, read [INSTALLATION.md](INSTALLATION.md). It distinguishes intended platforms from device combinations that have not been tested.

## Play

Click the course or hole map to set an aim point, or enter **Aim distance (yd)**. **Aim at pin** targets the cup. The bottom controls show distance to the pin and aim point. A manually selected aim distance stays fixed when changing power or club; arrow/controller aiming rotates it around the current lie. The initial target follows the middle of the fairway for the selected club’s stock carry, including through doglegs; near the end of the route or on the green it targets the pin. Thick animated dashes and small aiming markers travel from the ball toward the target in the scene and on the map; the shot tracer is thicker too. Choose a club, set power, and press **Space** or **Take your shot**. Arrow keys adjust aim and power; Q/E change clubs; C toggles the overview; Enter finishes an animation. On a standard gamepad, left stick adjusts aim/power, A/Cross shoots, shoulder buttons change clubs, Y/Triangle changes camera, and B/Circle skips flight or closes a panel. Press a controller button once so the browser can discover it. Actual controller hardware has not been tested in this environment.

Course studio offers Pacific Northwest, Desert, Mountain, Links, Midwest, Island, Giant Redwood, and Autumn landscapes. Giant Redwood is the moody one: ancient bare-trunked redwoods and douglas fir lining the fairways with fern underbrush, dark lush greens, and a sun low enough all day to rake light between the trunks and hang haze in the gaps. Every plant in it is generated rather than imported: coast redwoods running to 380 feet on fluted, buttressed trunks that stay bare for two thirds of their height, douglas fir and hemlock between them, a western red cedar and tanoak mid-storey, conifer seedlings, and a floor of sword fern, salal and redwood sorrel among nurse logs, stumps and moss-covered boulders. Their shapes and colours were measured from 315 photographs of real redwood forest. **The full scorecard is there before you build anything.** Course length, par and every tee's yardage sit in one box in the menu and in Course studio: move the length slider and the card redraws -- par per hole, out, in and total, blue, white and red. These are the yardages the course will actually have, not an estimate, because the card and the builder work the hole out the same way from the same seed. Hole lengths are held to real ranges for their par, so a par 5 cannot come out at 723 yards and starve the par 4s around it to pay for itself; ask for a length the pars cannot reach and the card tells you what it could reach instead. Par 3s and par 5s are spread across both nines rather than stacked on one.

Every seed creates an entire 9- or 18-hole course together in one shared landscape. Moving to the next hole keeps that landscape loaded. Water frequency, tree density, elevation, fairway width, green fringe width (0–6 m), semi-rough width (0–15 m), wind speed, and wind direction are adjustable. The ground is one continuous mesh with crisp fairway, semi-rough, fringe, green and sand boundaries and mowing stripes. Where a creek or river runs through a playing surface those turf edges soften and the mowing stripes fade into the bank, so the crossing reads as one gradient; this is the only place the boundaries are not crisp, and it changes appearance only — your lie is still decided at the true edge. **Graphics quality has four tiers**, and they are device-local -- a tier belongs to the machine you play on and never to the course, so two players on different settings play the identical hole. Low draws below native resolution and upscales, for a laptop with no graphics card; Ultra carries the largest shadows, reaching furthest, with bloom and the biggest reflections. Ground cues -- relief shading, slope tint, contours and mowing stripes -- are switches rather than tiers, because measured they cost nothing: nobody should drop to Low to turn a look off.

**The wind dial reads against the shot in front of you.** The arrow lies flat on the ground rather than printed on the glass, and it turns as you turn: straight up is wind going away from you, right is a crosswind running left to right. Leaves and dust drift past on the same wind, each one weaving on its own and trailing a small tail.

**Cartoon is the single art style**, with sculpted foliage, rough grass that leans downwind with gusts travelling across it (the semi-rough is mown turf and carries no blades — its cut height is the information), golden Links prairie, conifer pine-straw patches, drifting clouds and reflective water. Fairway edges vary independently. Course studio's **Fairway bunker occurrence** controls how often non-greenside bunkers sit inside the fairway; bunkers have excavated floors and local turf at the lip rather than a generic rough collar.

Course routing uses varied bearings and short green-to-tee transfers. **Course footprint** has 14 choices with selectable preview icons: Organic sweep, Elongated loop, Crescent, Winding ribbon, Original square, Figure eight, Butterfly, Three-leaf clover, Spiral, Horseshoe, Rounded triangle, Diamond, Coastal sweep and Island chain. These are guiding landscape curves, with seed-varied proportions and collision-checked placement; individual holes remain fully procedural. Woodland follows the routed corridors, and the detailed ground joins the distant landscape without an exposed rectangular tile edge. **Landscape character** controls inter-hole mountains, dunes, or island channels. **Space between hole corridors** controls routing clearance. **Dogleg frequency**, **Maximum dogleg turn**, and **Dogleg turning point** shape par 4/5 fairways. Par 3 centerlines are always straight, with restrained width changes. Left/right par 4/5 turns are balanced, and each pivot varies up to 22 percentage points earlier or later than the selected point. **Bunkers per hole** is an upper target; **Greenside bunker gap from fringe** allows close defensive placement, including zero gap. **Minimum/maximum water hazard depth** selects each lake's deepest point, with shallow banks and a local water elevation. Pond outlines have independently changing widths and curving banks that follow the neighboring fairway/semi-rough edge. Maps, water meshes, sand margins and collision basins share those bank profiles. Coastal channel floors respect the maximum too. These settings apply when you press **Generate & play**, rebuilding the whole landscape and starting a fresh round.

Elevation now has a much larger effect on playable corridors, with tens of metres of height change at high settings. Fairways have no finite outline families: each side uses 4–8 independently generated control points with smooth transitions, a variable broad taper, and local narrowing/widening. Green sizes, proportions and outlines, bunker outlines, fairway starts and hazard locations vary continuously. The width slider is a guideline. Par 3 fairways sometimes connect directly to their tee area. See [how procedural generation works](PROCEDURAL_GENERATION.md). Airborne leaves or light dust drift in the course-wide wind direction. Density increases gently with wind, is capped at 76 nearby particles, and is zero in calm conditions. Striking the ball adds a brief flash/ring and turf or sand spray, with a much lighter putting effect. These effects are visual and do not alter ball contact. Cartoon turf combines subtle procedural grain with wind-driven grass and canopy geometry, animated flags and slowly moving clouds.

Clouds drift on the course's own wind, thinning out as they leave and thickening as they arrive rather than appearing and vanishing whole. The shadow a cloud throws fades with it.

**Hole flyover** on the camera toolbar circles the whole hole from above — the same camera that orbits the showcase hole behind the main menu, at one and a half times its speed, one lap of about 56 seconds. It keeps well clear of ridges and treetops on any terrain. It returns to your ball and restores your green-reading overlays; the button, Escape, another camera or any panel stops it early.

**Typical fairway width** runs from 20 to 92 m — the top of that range is a hundred yards, which is links territory rather than a normal fairway. It is a guideline rather than a constant: each side of the corridor is generated independently, so a hole widens into its landing areas and pinches at the approach.

**Blind tee shots** sets how often a hole is allowed to keep one. A tee is normally raised until the shot clears whatever stands in front of it; this is the share of holes left alone instead. Only holes the land actually makes blind can be chosen, so the real rate tops out near one in five however high it goes. The default is none.

**Elevation severity** at zero is a baseline rather than a promise of dead level: the ground behind a tee keeps its own relief so a tee has real ground to be cut into, and elevation raises the playing corridor from there. The driving range is the exception and stays perfectly flat, because any tilt there is a variable you did not set, quietly added to every carry.

**Typical pond size** scales both pond dimensions from 40–300 m. Because pond banks are anchored outside the fairway edge, a larger pond grows away from play rather than into it. **Large lakes** requests 0–3 larger water bodies, with a **Typical lake diameter** of 60–460 m. Ordinary ponds are substantial too, typically spanning around 90 m; lakes are for genuinely large water. At the top of the lake range, open ground runs out and fewer lakes are placed than requested. Placement favors open, gentler ground and may produce fewer lakes where there is insufficient room. They use the existing minimum/maximum water depth. River and creek banks blend biome-specific damp earth, grass and stone colors into the surrounding turf. Channels descend through smooth terrain cutouts and share the ponds’ reflective water. Water is translucent and thins out at a channel’s edge instead of stopping against the bank; pond, lake and ocean edges do not thin. Every pond, lake and channel is the same surface, and reflects its own surroundings from a small cubemap taken where that body sits when the course is built. There is no single mirror handed between bodies any more, so water never changes character as you walk past it.

Tees are sited rather than placed. Each pad tries a few dozen nearby positions once the land exists and takes the one the ground already suits — flat, with a clear view, sitting above the tee in front of it, and never on a green, in water, in sand or on another hole. That is where the variety comes from: a back tee on a rise, a forward tee tucked in beside the fairway, tees at genuinely different heights. A pad is a 6 by 9 m rectangle with rounded corners, squared to as far down the hole as a straight line from that tee stays inside the corridor — the dogleg corner where there is one, the landing area where there is not. Short holes get three ordinary tees stepping down, not one long shared box.

Rivers and creeks are read off the land's own drainage rather than drawn across it. The whole course is solved once — depressions flooded to their spill height, every cell pointed at its steepest lower neighbour, and the drainage accumulated — so a creek is a tributary of the river rather than an independent squiggle. Water never crosses a fairway, a green or another body of water, and a channel that reaches a hollow ends in a pond built like any other.

Every biome now mixes several plant forms: conifers/alder/ferns; desert trees/cacti/rosettes; conifers/aspen; grasses/heath/gorse; parkland broadleaf trees; palms/hala/coastal shrubs; or mixed fall woodland. See [landscape research and aerial references](LANDSCAPE_RESEARCH.md) for the sources and design decisions.

**Course length** sets target blue-tee yardage, with a live total par and par-3/4/5 breakdown. Total par stays at 68–72 for 18 holes and 34–36 for nine; at the extremes, distance continues changing while par stays within those limits. The seed randomizes the par mix and hole order. The target supports 990–4,230 yards for nine holes and 1,980–8,460 for eighteen. Individual rounded hole yardages can make the total differ by a few yards. All courses include **Blue, White and Red tees**, with actual pads, markers, independently varied forward positions, and small lateral offsets. Choose **Playing tee** in the bottom controls or round setup; tee selection is separate from generation. Changing it before the first stroke moves you to that tee; after a stroke, the preference applies on the next hole. White tees average roughly 9% shorter and red roughly 18%. Scorecards and CSV exports include all three yardage rows.

**Green slope & difficulty** ranges from level greens at 0% to genuinely severe ones at 100%: broad tilt, crossing ridges, a punchbowl or a crowned turtleback depending on the seed, and a tier whose face is the steepest ground on the surface. The scale is deliberately uneven — the default of 35% sits where it always has, around 1.3 ft of relief, while 100% carries about 5.6 ft with tier faces near 18%, matching the greens tournament players see. Even at the top, part of every green stays flat enough to cut a hole on. **Pin difficulty** cuts the cups for a day of a tournament: Thursday takes the flattest ground with the most room, and Friday through Sunday move to progressively more slope. No day will cut a cup where a ball cannot come to rest, or nearer than about three paces to an edge. Hole locations work front, middle, back and around again as the round goes on, and the hole card shows which you are playing. Rendering and ball contact use the same ground heights. **Green reading** is three independent tools, each a small button along the top of the course map: a slope-colored 1.5 m grid; animated markers that originate at grid vertices and follow actual downhill gradients; and an elevation heat map with 10 cm contour lines. Blue/green/yellow/orange/red grid colors indicate increasing slope. Heat map blue is lower elevation and red is higher on that green. Use any combination. They sit on the map because that is what they describe, and they can be switched at any time — you no longer open a panel to reach them. These display controls need no regeneration and remain separate from Stimp. They are off and disabled on the driving range, where the practice green is deliberately flat and every overlay would paint one featureless colour.

**The map follows the ball onto the green.** Once the ball is on the putting surface the map stops showing four hundred yards of fairway you have finished with, frames the green alone and paints the same contour field from straight above — blue low, red high, lines every 10 cm. Read from the ball, the far half of a green is a few pixels tall, which is exactly the half you are trying to judge; from overhead it is legible. It is the same height field the 3D overlay draws and the same field the putt rolls on. Step off the green and the hole map comes back.

**The pin comes out when you are putting**, the way it does on a real green, so there is nothing standing between the ball and the cup while you read the line. The cup stays. With the flagstick gone the floating marker names the hole rather than the pin, and it no longer drifts off the edge of the screen when you turn away: it pins itself to the nearest clear edge, points back at the cup, and keeps the distance readable. It clears the shot controls and the hole map rather than hiding behind them, and it follows those panels if you drag them. The putt line rides on the green itself rather than floating above the ball, and a putter is only selected for you when the ball is actually on the putting surface -- from the fringe or a greenside bunker you get a club to chip with.

**Saved courses** is its own place on the main menu: play anything you have kept, save the landscape you are on, and move courses in and out — as a short code for one course, or as a file holding the whole library. Importing a file adds to what you have rather than replacing it, and a course you already have is recognised and skipped, so re-importing a backup does not give you two of everything. **Saved rounds** live in the round dialogue's own tab, beside the export and import that make them.

Use the player avatar or format button to create a 1–4 player, 9/18-hole local round. Stroke play keeps each golfer active until they finish the hole. Scramble rotates through teammates and lets the team select a lie; only the chosen result's penalty is counted. Match play uses singles or two best-ball teams and finishes when the lead exceeds remaining holes. Match teams must each have at least one golfer. Match play does not implement alternate-shot/foursomes or handicaps.

Putting offers three modes, set in two places that share one copy of the setting: the **Format & tees** section of the round panel, where the mode and its distances sit inline with format and tees, and *Putting options* in the tools tray during a round. Both write the same choice; the round-panel fields apply as soon as they are changed. **Hole out** requires actual cup capture, with a 107.95 mm diameter cup, a floor 101.6 mm down — the four inches the USGA requires — and a 42.67 mm ball. The rim is a surface the ball genuinely runs on rather than a rule about speed: a putt can catch the lip, ride it round — a full lap on a quick green — and either drop or come back out at you, and how long it rides depends on the green's Stimp, because the lip costs the ball what its own turf costs it. Dead centre the cup catches a ball arriving at about 1.75 m/s. There is no gimme distance. A close putting camera activates when playing from a green; the white locator disappears within 5 m of the cup. **Dartboard** awards 1, 2, or 3 putts only after a shot stops on the current green. **Decimal** interpolates between the three adjustable distances and records two decimal places. At 2/8/16-yard thresholds, a 5-yard leave adds 1.50 putts and a 12-yard leave adds 2.50. The shot itself also counts. Both automatic modes cap at three putts outside the outer circle. Rings are visible only where they lie on the green. In scramble, players choose a candidate before its automatic putts are applied. Existing scores are not recalculated when the mode changes.

**Turf firmness** — Burnt, Firm, Normal or Soft — is not the same thing as green speed. It decides what a ball does the moment it lands: how high it bounces and whether it holds. The four settings are the USGA's own published GS3 reference bands, measured as how far a dropped ball penetrates the surface in inches, so "Firm" means what a superintendent means by it. A 7-iron into a green runs about 4 yards on soft turf and 26 on burnt, with the same carry. Green speed and the roll settings decide what happens once it is already rolling, so a green can be quick and soft, or slow and baked hard.

**My bag** edits every club's full-power carry (putter: roll). Launch speed is calibrated to the entered distance on level ground in still air at sea level. **Ball & turf** saves speed, launch, spin and spin-axis biases, with high/low/draw/fade presets; **Shot shape** adds a temporary adjustment. Wind, elevation and lie can still change actual distance. These manual profiles leave measured monitor shots unchanged.

**Sim drop** opens at the current lie. Click terrain or the map, enter sideways/along-hole offsets in yards, then choose **Place ball** or **Cancel**. There is no penalty. Scramble drops move the whole team from its shared lie; finish a pending team selection first. Drops can reach anywhere inside the generated course boundary. **Replay last shot** plays the recorded flight or putt without changing the ball’s current lie, player, scores, or undo history. It also works after moving to the next hole, until course regeneration or a fresh round. Replay is session-only and is cleared on reload; it uses the original recorded path rather than resimulating with changed settings. Replaying pauses automatic scorecard advancement. **Mulligan** restores the previous shot's lie, player, penalty, scores and team/match state, and takes that shot's tracer off the hole with it — the shots before it stay. It reaches back only into **the hole you are playing**: on a fresh tee there is nothing of your own to take back yet and the button says so. Up to 30 shots are retained, including across reloads. Shots made in older versions have no undo history.

**Panels move and resize at all times**, with no mode to enter first. Drag the course card, weather, course map, shot controls, tools tray, results or free-flight controls by the grip in the corner, and pull the opposite corner to resize. Focus a handle and use arrow keys for 10-pixel adjustments, or Shift+arrows for 1 pixel. Layout saves separately on this browser, adapts to screen size, and **Reset panel layout** in the tools tray restores the defaults. Panels can scroll when resized small.

Building a course takes a few seconds and the page cannot respond while it happens, so a **"Growing your landscape…"** message covers the wait. Nine holes are quicker than eighteen, and trees, houses and waterways all add to it.

Fairway opens on a **starting screen** — the mark on black while the renderer comes up and the first hole grows — and then on a **main menu**, with the camera circling a single showcase hole — a different one each visit, at a different time of day, and floodlit when the hour it picks is dark; nothing is generated for you and no round starts until you ask. From there: continue the round you were playing, **Play**, or open **Course studio**. Play chooses a saved course, your group, the format and your tee, and is also where you **delete** a course you no longer want or reach **Manage & share courses** — the course decides the landscape and how many holes it has, so there is nothing about terrain to set here. **Surprise me & play** skips the choosing and builds a fresh randomised nine on the spot.

**Driving range** is a practice ground rather than a hole: 500 yards deep, 100 yards wide, dead flat, and mown from behind the mats all the way to the back of the field. It is the same range every visit — nothing about it is drawn from a seed — so two shots hit a week apart are comparable. There is no round and no score; the only thing it gives back is what the ball did. A real green sits out on the centre line, and the **Green distance** slider in the tools tray slides it anywhere from 30 to 300 yards so you can practise a particular number — the painted surface, the flag, the cup and every distance readout move with it, and the field stays mown to 500 yards behind wherever it ends up. Six targets run down the field at 50 to 300 yards, alternating sides, each a coloured circle with a white rim, an oversized flag in the same colour and a board behind it carrying the number — the colour tells you which one you are looking at before you read the board, and the boards grow with distance so the far ones stay legible from the mats. The targets are markings rather than putting surfaces: a ball landing on one takes a range-turf bounce, and the movable green is the one that plays as a green.

Hitting is a loop: the ball returns to the mat after every shot, the club you picked stays in your hands, and the tracers stay on the field behind you so a session reads as a session. **Shot lines** in the range controls decides how many of those tracers stay up — anywhere from none, for a clear field, to the last 50 — and it applies as you drag it. A session always keeps the full 50 behind the scenes, so raising the slider brings back lines you have already hit rather than only affecting the next shot. Nothing is scored — the hole card just says Range, par N/A, and the live distance to the green. Everything the shot did goes to **Shot information**: carry, total, offline, apex, ball speed, launch angle, spin and spin axis, with a running average carry and offline spread across the session. Offline is measured across the line you aimed down rather than from the green, so aiming at a target and hitting it counts as straight. Every shot is recorded against the golfer who hit it, and clicking the shot number on a player's card — or **Shot data** in the tools tray — opens that golfer's session list. The lab records into the same list, naming each row by what fired it: the preset, the launch-monitor line, or the putt distance. Every row has a **Replay** button that flies that shot again — the trajectory that actually happened, not a fresh simulation, so moving the green or changing the firmness afterwards cannot change what you are shown. The last 50 shots of a session stay replayable; older rows keep their numbers.

**Lab tools** is a popup in the driving range, not a mode of its own. It carries three things: **green firmness**, which decides what a ball does the moment it lands — how high it bounces and whether it holds; **green speed** in Stimp, which decides what it does once it is rolling; and a box to **fire a shot** from typed numbers rather than a swing. Give it ball speed, launch angle, direction, spin and spin axis — or paste Open Connect JSON straight from a launch monitor — and that shot goes through the same simulator a swing goes through. Nothing is invented: the five numbers you give are the five numbers the ball leaves with, and the shot is recorded in your shot data as *Launch monitor*. Both turf settings apply immediately, to the shot in front of you and every one after it.

There used to be a separate **Lab** mode with its own flat green, a floating control bar and sixteen preset shots to click. The range replaced the need for it — it is already flat, mown end to end, and its green moves to any stated number — so the mode is gone and what was actually being reached for is a popup. The measurement half survives in the browser console as a `lab` object: `lab.batch`, `lab.drops`, `lab.scatter`, `lab.lipSweep` and `lab.envelope` run sweeps against the loaded course without animating them, which is how carry, release, firmness and bounce behaviour were fitted in the first place. Time can still be slowed to a twentieth and the camera can still sit on the turf beside the cup.

The main menu stays behind whichever dialogue you open from it — the wordmark and the landscape remain, and the mode tiles step aside for the sheet that replaced them. The round dialogue offers **Start fresh round** only when you are not already playing one; opened mid-round it is there to change your group, reach your saved rounds and export the one in progress, and starting over is a main-menu decision. Every mode opens the same way: a tabbed dialogue that asks what you want before anything is built, with the way in — *Start fresh round*, *Start an endless run*, *Open the driving range*, *Grow this landscape* — at the right-hand end of the tab row, so it is on screen whichever tab you are reading.

On the range the map also carries **dispersion**: one ellipse per club, drawn from the shots you actually hit, with a dot for every finish and the group's centre marked. It covers every ball that club hit, and it lies along the way the club actually misses rather than along the aim line — long and short is a different miss from left and right, and most clubs group about half as wide as they are deep. The legend gives both axes, long first. A club needs three shots before it draws. It is a read of what happened and not a model of what might: the simulator adds no miss of its own, so a club fed identical numbers ten times groups on a point. Toggle it and read the shot counts in Range controls.

The **course card** carries the hole, the scores and the live shot readout in one panel, with the course seed in its top-right corner — click it to copy. Panels share one set of corners and shadows, and text follows one rule: a serif face for the course title, panel headings and the large numbers you glance at, and a sans face for every label, button and table.

**Every panel on screen moves and resizes, whenever you want.** The course card, the weather, the map, the shot controls, the tools tray and the shot information each carry a small grip in one corner and a resize handle in the other — drag the grip, pull the corner, or focus either and use the arrow keys. Where you put them is remembered, in every mode. There is no arrange mode to enter; *Reset panel layout* in the tools tray puts everything back.

The **hole map** scrolls and zooms in every mode, the studio included: the wheel zooms about the pointer, dragging pans, and a double-click returns it to the fitted view. The title shows the zoom factor whenever the map is not showing the whole hole or the whole course. Clicking still aims — a drag is not treated as a click — and the map returns to its fitted view whenever a new hole loads.

**Shot views** draws the shot flat, in the two shapes the 3D view cannot make: height against distance down the line, and the ground track across it. It is a tool you open rather than something on screen while you hit. The lower plot's dashed centre line is the line you aimed down, not the line to the green, so a shot aimed at a target and hit straight reads straight. It follows your last shot on its own; pressing **View** on a row in your shot data pins it to that shot until you hit the next one.

**Range controls** stands open the whole time you are on the range, with the green distance and shot-line sliders in it — nothing to go looking for. **Shot shape** holds both halves of how the ball leaves the club: spin axis, launch adjustment and spin adjustment for the shot in front of you, and below them the ball flight that stays with you across clubs and rounds. The per-shot spin is in rpm, because you are hitting one known club; the profile's is a percentage, because it scales a bag whose clubs spin anywhere from 2,700 to 9,000. **Turf settings** holds the ground it all lands on — green speed, the roll settings and firmness.

**Growing a course no longer freezes the page.** The generator hands the browser a frame as it works, so the waiting card turns, names what it is doing — routing the holes, shaping the land, cutting the water, building the ground, planting — and fills a bar that means something: the ground alone is four fifths of the job, and the reading is weighted so it advances evenly rather than crawling and then leaping. It costs about a quarter more time overall than locking the page solid did.

**A hole can be given something to play around.** *Feature tree or rocks in a fairway* sets how often a hole gets a specimen standing in its own short grass — the lone tree, the outcrop of stones — the way a famous hole often does. It is sited to be fair rather than cheap: there is always open grass down at least one side, it keeps well back from the green, it only appears where the hole is wide enough to take one, and it never stands close enough to a tee to leave you no shot. Set it to zero for clean fairways.

**Boulders stop a ball.** Rocks are part of the course now rather than scenery painted on it, so a stone in the rough is something to play around instead of something to roll through — they run up to six metres across on mountain and desert. Floodlight masts stop a ball too, but only when the lights are actually on and the masts are standing there to be seen. Fallen logs and stumps in the redwoods are still scenery.

**Green shape** and **Bunker shape** set how far a green or a bunker departs from an oval. Everything used to be a smooth oval; turned up, greens grow lobes and pinched waists and sand goes ragged. Raising a slider also evens out the waves that build the outline, so no single one takes over — letting one dominate is what turns a green into a tidy three-lobed flower instead of something a groundsman would recognise. Put either back to zero and you get the rounded outlines the game always had. One thing to know: shaping a green makes the ground around it steeper, noticeably so on hilly settings.

**Trees close to greens** sets how far planting comes in around a green. The side your shot comes in from stays clear at every setting — there is always somewhere to land — and the slider moves the trees behind and to the sides. At zero they stand well back; at full they close in on the collar, so a green can sit in a clearing or in a pocket of woodland.

**Tees sit in the trees, not in a clearing.** That holds however wide you set the fairways — the planting round a tee is measured against the hole it belongs to rather than a fixed distance, so a wide course does not end up with its tees in clearings. Planting comes right up to the mown apron behind and beside a tee box, so it is framed by whatever grows on that course instead of standing in a bare circle. Only the ground in front is kept open — and it is judged from the whole tee complex at once, so a tree cannot sit in the view from the back tee just because it misses that tee’s own line — and tree lines everywhere thin out towards the short grass rather than stopping along a hard line.

**You always have a shot from the tee.** Nothing solid is allowed to stand where your tee shot would fly through it — not a trunk, a boulder or a fallen log. It is judged on the line the hole actually asks for, so a par three is measured to the green rather than to some notional drive, and on height as well as distance: a tall tree is refused far down the hole, while a rock the ball sails thirty metres over is left where it is. Only the ground in FRONT of a tee is kept clear — trees beside and behind the box are deliberately left, because they are what makes a tee look like somewhere rather than a platform.

**A new hole arrives.** Reaching a hole you have not played — teeing off a round, walking to the next hole, or the next hole of an endless run — opens above and behind the tee, looking down the length of it. It holds there for a couple of seconds so you can read the shape and where the trouble is, then flies down onto your ball. Resuming a hole you are already halfway down skips it, and so does the driving range.

**The camera flies between views rather than cutting.** Moving to the overview or the green view and back, stepping out of a flyover, and every way into a round — an endless run, a new course, continuing a saved round, the driving range, or coming down out of the studio — are all flown: the camera lifts, crosses, and settles on the shot, easing out of the move and into it so it starts and stops at rest. It keeps above the ground and above the treetops on the way, and short hops behind the ball are left alone rather than turned into a journey.

**The camera** stays with you for a moment and a half after the strike before it goes after the ball — a putt it follows immediately, because the roll is the whole event. It trails from off your shoulder rather than from straight behind, and along the line to the hole rather than the line you struck on, so the flag stays in shot on anything that curves. The closer the ball works toward the hole the tighter it frames, all the way down to turf level for a ball trickling up to the cup.

**Morning fog & haze** and **Floodlight the course** sit in the clock panel beside the time controls — the first of the weather settings. Floodlights turn on night golf. Poles run down alternating sides of every hole, with a pair flanking the back of every green at 45° either side of the line you play in on, placed the way a sports field is lit — 23 m masts, spaced within three times their own height, set 18 yd beyond the semi rough along the fairway and 10 yd past it around the greens, so they are never in play. Off by default: nothing is built into the skyline and nothing is lit until you ask for it.

**Endless** plays the hole you are already looking at. Choosing it asks for your format, tees, putting mode and group first — there is no course to pick, since every hole is grown from the run's own seed, and match play is not offered because a match ends when the lead exceeds the holes remaining and an endless run has no last hole. The showcase hole on the menu is hole one of a real endless run, so starting one simply tees it up — no wait, no regeneration — and each time you hole out the next hole grows from the same seed. A run is one seed and a hole number, so it can be put down and picked up later without storing a course.

**Course studio** is its own mode. Fly over the landscape with W/A/S/D, open **Settings** to change anything about how it grows, then press **Regenerate** to rebuild it. Settings are grouped as Course, Landscape, Routing, Fairways &amp; greens, Bunkers, Ponds &amp; lakes, Rivers &amp; creeks, Trees &amp; scenery and Weather, and every one has an **ⓘ** explaining what it changes — or press **Show all descriptions** to read the lot. Nothing rebuilds on its own — a course takes seconds to grow — so the bar tells you when settings have changed and the landscape is out of date. **Save course** names it and puts it in your library. **Menu** in the top bar returns here at any time.

**Course studio → Saved courses** keeps a library of landscapes on this device. Name a course you like and it is there next time; **Play** puts you straight back on it, and **Rename** changes what it is called without touching the landscape. The save button names what it would save — *Save "Midwest"* while you are playing it, *Save this landscape* in the studio — and when there is nothing to save, at the main menu or during an endless run, it says so and offers Course studio instead. A saved course holds its landscape only, so the same course works for any group, format or tee. **Get code** turns one into a short `FW1.…` code you can paste to someone else, and **Import code** adds theirs to your library — it carries no scores, no round in progress, and none of your own club or turf settings. A code from a newer Fairway is refused rather than half-read, and a course built by a different generator is flagged in the list.

**Continue and Saved rounds are two different things.** *Continue* on the main menu picks up the one round you were last playing — it keeps itself, with no naming and no list, and there is only ever one. *Saved rounds*, in the round dialogue's own tab, is the shelf you put rounds on deliberately: leaving a round you have started offers to save it there under a name, and each one resumes to the same lie and the same scores. Choosing **leave and let it go** really does let it go — the round is not waiting for you the next time you open Fairway. The showcase hole behind the main menu is never saved as a round.

**A round remembers how you were playing it, not just where the ball was.** Club distances, flight profile and turf conditions travel with a saved, exported and resumed round, endless runs included — an endless run grows its ground from a seed, but your bag is yours and comes with you.

**Each saved round exports on its own**, from its row in the list, under a filename taken from its name — and each can be renamed there too. The separate *Export the round you are playing* is for the one in your hands, and is offered only while there is one.

**Importing a round is entering it.** If you are in the middle of a round, Fairway asks whether to save that one first, exactly as leaving by any other route does; once the file is read you are put straight into the imported round rather than left on the menu.

Saved and exported rounds record which course generator built them. When a Fairway update changes how courses grow, reopening an older round asks whether to continue on the rebuilt landscape or start a fresh round on the same course, rather than quietly moving the ground under your ball. Scores are kept either way. Settings themselves are upgraded automatically, so an older save never has to be rebuilt by hand.

Water and out-of-bounds still incur one penalty stroke and replay from the previous lie. This simplified stroke-and-distance rule applies to water; lateral-drop and full tournament rules are not implemented.

**Camera & bay** is on the main menu as well as in the tools tray, and everything in it stays on this device — it survives a reload, and it is never carried inside a course or a round you share.

**Simulator bay** turns the field of view from a look into a measurement. Tell it your screen size and shape and how far back you stand, and it works out the one vertical angle that makes the course line up with the room: the screen is a window, and there is exactly one angle that makes what is on it match what would be there if the wall were not. With it on, the camera stands where you stand — directly behind the ball with no sideways offset at all, at your eye height, with the ball a real distance in front of you. The ball can sit below the bottom of the frame; it is below your eyeline in the room too. The panel shows the angle your bay gives, and for a hand-set angle it tells you how far back you would have to stand to justify it. This assumes the projector fills the screen and the browser fills the projector.

**Reading the ground** is four switches in Graphics, because a cast shadow only tells you anything when the sun is low. **Ground shading** is a raking light on a fixed bearing plus a lift on crowns and a shade in hollows, worked out when the course is built — it is what makes a fairway read as a surface rather than a sheet. **Slope tinting** dries out the turf on slopes and keeps hollows lush, the way water actually runs off; it is the only one of the three that works in colour, so it still reads when the others are competing with the sun. **Contour lines** draw a topographic line every metre of height across the whole course — frankly artificial, and the easiest of the three to read, because it turns a slope into a spacing you can count. **Mowing stripes** are the fourth: alternating cut bands that bend over a roll and change contrast with the slope, the way real ones do because the mower follows the ground. Everything here is on by default except contours, and none of it costs a measurable frame.

**Costs a frame** is the group below it, and they are labelled that way because they are real work on every frame: **terrain casts shadows** (ridges shading the hollows behind them, which only says anything when the sun is low) and **terrain casts shadows** alone. **Cloud shadows** (high and ultra) belong to the clouds you can actually see overhead rather than to a noise field: each cloud's disc is projected along the sun, so the patch of shade sits offset from the cloud the way a real one does, follows the terrain for free and drifts with the same wind. It adds no render pass — it darkens direct sunlight inside the materials that are already being drawn, and leaves ambient alone, because a cloud blocks the sun and not the sky. **Water reflections** moved out of this group because it no longer costs a frame: it chooses what each body's cubemap holds — the course, or the sky by itself — and the capture happens once, while the course is being built. The surface is the same either way: clear where you look into it, sky where you look across it, and ripples generated from the world position rather than sampled from a texture, so there is no tile to spot at pond scale.

**On the green the camera backs off far enough to show your ball.** You aim a putt from the ball, so it has to be on screen — off the green it can sit below the bottom of the frame, because that is where it is when you are standing over it. The camera keeps the same eye height, the same line and the same field of view and simply stands further back; the hole stays in frame too. On the default broadcast camera nothing moves at all, because the ball is already in shot.

**A putt is followed with the camera pointed at the hole.** It is the same camera as every other shot — same height, same distance, same angle — but the look is pinned to the cup while the ball rolls, so the hole stays still on screen instead of drifting around it while you watch to see whether it drops.

**Which view you are looking through is on the tools tray, not in the settings.** Behind the ball, whole-course overview, a look at the green and free flight are four buttons that are on screen the whole time, and each toggles back to the ball view. The camera panel is only for how the view is set up — it has no view picker of its own, because the view you are currently looking through is not a setting.

Camera settings adjust height above the ball, distance behind it, horizontal offset and field of view — or, with **Simulator bay** on, they take your bay's measurements instead. There is no separate putting camera: the view you set up is the view you keep, on the green as much as anywhere. Horizontal offset translates both the camera and its target equally, preserving the viewing direction. Press **V** or the bird icon for free flight: **W/A/S/D** moves, **R/F** rises/descends, **Shift** boosts speed, and dragging or arrow keys turns the camera. Scroll to fly forward/backward. Jump to any green using the hole selector, click the whole-course map, or use the on-screen movement buttons. **Return to ball**, **V**, or **C** restores the appropriate ball view, including the close putting camera on a green, without changing the current shot. Distance to pin stays in the bottom controls (and free-flight bar), using feet close to the cup and yards farther away. Camera settings also include free-flight speed. The round automatically saves after completed shots. Export/import a saved round to transfer it to another browser, and download the scorecard as CSV. File-URL storage behavior varies by browser; use export for a reliable portable save.

When a hole is holed out, the camera pulls back into a slow orbit of the whole hole with **every tracer of that hole drawn at once**, so you can see the way it was played while the scorecard is up. During play a tracer clears once its shot is over rather than hanging over the next one.

The course card shows a **live score**: whose turn it is and how they stand against par, tinted behind the number — progressively red over par, green under. The scorecard shows the same for every player. The hole card beside it shows par, which pin day the cups are cut for and whether this hole is front, middle or back.

**A scramble chooses its ball on the course, not from a list.** Once every teammate has hit, all of the team's balls stay on the course where they lie, each in the colour of the golfer who hit it and each under a beam so it can be found from the far side of a fairway. The bar at the foot of the screen steps between them, putting the camera behind each ball looking down its line to the pin — the view the next shot would actually be played from — and the check mark takes the one you are looking at. It opens on the ball nearest the pin, which is usually but not always the one you want. A list could tell you "Alex, 148 yd"; it could not tell you that Alex is behind a bunker.

**Any golfer can be removed from a round in progress**, including the one whose turn it is and including the first name in the list. The Apply button names who is leaving and asks for a second press — their scorecard goes with them and there is no undo. Whoever is left keeps their own card, their own strokes and their own ball; the turn passes on if the golfer who was up has gone.

**In stroke play each golfer plays the hole out before the next one starts**, and the next tee is played in the order they scored the last one — **lowest score has honours**. Golfers who tied keep the order they already had, and anyone who joined mid-round and has no score for that hole goes to the back. Best-ball match play still plays the ball farthest from the pin shot by shot, with honours breaking the tie on the tee where every ball is the same distance away. Scramble teams alternate from their shared lie, as they always have.

**Every golfer in the group has a colour** — gold, cyan, indigo, magenta, by their position in the group. It is the chip beside their name on the course card, the dot on their scorecard row, the dot on their row in the group editor, and the colour of their tracer on the end-of-hole flyover, so a line over the fairway and a name on the card are recognisably the same person. Red and green are never used for a golfer: those belong to over and under par on the same cards.

Three seconds after everyone finishes a hole, the scorecard shows each score and putt total, including fractional automatic putts. It advances after eight seconds; **Stay on scorecard** pauses it, and **Next hole now** skips the wait. Final rounds stay on the final card. Scorecard CSVs include putts. Putting statistics for shots from older saves were not recorded and cannot be reconstructed.

**Ball & turf** also sets green Stimp (6–15 ft) and fairway/semi-rough/rough roll (30–180%). Higher percentages produce more run. Defaults are Stimp 10 and 100% roll. Putter bag distance is calibrated at Stimp 10; faster/slower greens change its real distance. Turf conditions affect measured monitor shots as well as manual shots.

## Homes, waterways and presentation

Enable **Line fairways with houses** in Course studio and adjust **House occurrence** and **House setback from semi-rough**. Houses face the playing corridor, use regional colors, and occupy dry, gentle rough away from greens and tee pads. Trees stay clear of their footprints. Houses are solid: a ball rebounds off walls and roofs and stays in play, and one resting against a wall can still be played away from it. Turn on **Houses play as out of bounds** in Course studio if you would rather reaching one cost a penalty stroke and a replay from your previous lie; it is off unless you choose it. Houses vary their roof form, porches, garages, fences and planting, sit on foundations cut to their own ground, and are outlined on the full-course map. Constrained sites may produce fewer houses.

**Rivers** (0–2) and **Creeks** (0–3) add meandering channels that can cross fairways or run between holes. Adjust their independent widths, channel depth and meandering. Creek depth is 60% of the chosen river depth. Each channel draws its own bearing and meander wavelengths, so rivers and creeks no longer run as parallel copies of one curve. Channels sweep through a fairway as one continuous curve rather than turning corners around whatever they avoid, and they route around bunkers; on a course too tight to route around one, the channel washes that bunker out rather than leaving water standing in sand. Protected tee, green, pond and bunker areas may reduce the number of accepted channels. Channels have carved beds, actual water lies, animated water and map lines, and a staged shoreline of wet margin and damp earth that widens with the channel and fades into the surrounding ground. Mown turf is kept close to the water; rough and open ground keep a much broader bank. Their water profile follows the terrain as a visual approximation; this is not a drainage simulation and bridges are not included yet.

The ground is levelled into a shelf beneath each pond during generation, so a pond no longer needs a naturally flat site; it still sits below its rim and outer banks, with fine shore and bed geometry. Wherever a pond, lake, river or creek meets a fairway, the mown corridor stops behind a full band of semi-rough that comes round the shoreline, starting where the damp shore soil ends so the whole width of it is visible. The band is the hole's own semi-rough width, so it widens and narrows with that setting and disappears entirely at zero. The turf reads as mown around the hazard rather than sliced off at it — and a channel crossing a fairway ends the mown corridor on one bank and restarts it on the other instead of running underneath. Ponds can sit well out in the rough, come up to the playing corridor, bite into it, or cross it completely and split the hole into two landing areas — water carried off the tee is a shape golf uses and the generator can now produce it. Splits are uncommon (about 6% of holes at the default water setting, 13% at the maximum) and the carry is a golf shot rather than a wall: 33 yards typically, 101 at the most extreme measured. Ponds, lakes, rivers and creeks are cut into the ground the way bunkers are: the water sits about a metre below its rim, the ground is held out to that rim and then drops over a couple of metres, and a lip of wet soil meets the turf. **The ocean is the exception** — a coast is not an excavation, so island and links shores get a shaped foreshore and a real beach that is painted as sand and plays as sand, wide where the shore is flat and narrow where it is steep. **Island courses carry no inland water** — no ponds, lakes, rivers or creeks, whatever those settings say. The ocean is the hazard, it is already in play on most shots, and a strip of land with sea on every side is the worst possible place to site a pond. The settings keep their values, so switching a course to island and back returns the water it had. On Links, which also has a coast, water is kept on land: a channel is trimmed at the shoreline and fades out where it meets the sea rather than being drawn along the ocean floor, and a channel is chosen for how much of the course it crosses rather than for how long it is. They share the same wet-margin and damp-earth shoreline as rivers and creeks rather than a sand ring. At high **Water frequency** settings large ponds crowd each other, so about one pond per hole is placed however many are requested. Green surroundings blend over a much broader area to soften steep shoulders. Green difficulty continues to control the putting surface itself.

The shot-result panel remains open, including during free flight. During a shot it displays live horizontal distance from the launch point and height above the terrain. A replay holds the final lie for **three seconds** before returning. Taking a putt animates the flag upward out of the cup. Hole completion leaves the cup visible for **three seconds** before opening the scorecard; its existing eight-second next-hole countdown starts afterward. Explicit skip/continue controls remain available.

## Launch monitor setup

The browser cannot listen to raw TCP or automatically decode proprietary Bluetooth hardware. The included Node bridge receives the **Open Connect v1** format used by community launch-monitor connectors and relays it over WebSocket. No commercial simulator installation is required to run Fairway itself. Device software and connector requirements still apply.

1. Run `npm ci`, `npm run build`, and `npm run bridge` in this folder.
2. Open http://127.0.0.1:1922 (or the standalone HTML file).
3. In Fairway's **Connect monitor** panel, connect to `ws://127.0.0.1:1922` and check **Arm monitor for live shots**.
4. Configure your launch monitor's existing Open Connect connector to send to **127.0.0.1, TCP port 1921**. Set the connector’s destination port explicitly. If it is fixed to the standard port 921, run the bridge with FAIRWAY_TCP_PORT=921 on a host that permits it (Windows normally does; macOS/Linux may require a privileged-port forwarding rule). Fairway defaults to 1921 so no administrator access is needed.
5. Select the club and aim in Fairway. Shots are played from measured speed, launch angles, and spin. Manual shot power, lie penalties to launch speed, and shot-shape settings do not override measured monitor data.

The panel also validates and plays sample JSON independently of hardware. This sample is a real physics input, not evidence of a connected device. Bridge connectivity and device connectivity are reported separately. Incoming shots are rejected while unarmed, during flight, or while a hole/lie selection needs attention. There is no silent shot queue. The bridge sends the monitor a success only after the browser accepts the shot. Club/handedness messages use code 201; the community ready extension uses 202. Duplicate device/shot-number pairs are rejected within each browser connection.

Two markers float in the world rather than in a bar: the **flag** shows yards to the pin, and the **aim point** shows how far away the spot you are aiming at is. The aim marker steps aside when it would sit on top of the flag, and disappears once the ball is on the green — there the distance to the pin is the number that matters, and the putt preview line already shows where the ball finishes.

With a monitor connected, the club card becomes a state light: **red** for no device, **amber** while the monitor hunts for a ball, **green** the moment it has one, each labelled in words as well as colour. Arm the monitor and the shot controls strip back to the club alone — ball speed, launch, direction, spin and axis all come off the device, so aim buttons, a power slider and a swing button decide nothing. Aim is still set by clicking the map or the course. This applies in every mode that shows the shot bar, the driving range included.

If a connector will not talk, run `npm run bridge:debug` (or set `FAIRWAY_LOG=debug`) for per-message logging: every raw payload in, every reply code out, and each shot decoded into mph and rpm, which is where wrong units and swapped spin fields actually show up. Defaults bind both servers to loopback, and only local/file browser origins are accepted. One browser controls the bridge at a time. Ports can be set with FAIRWAY_TCP_PORT and FAIRWAY_HTTP_PORT. If a connector runs on another machine (for example, a Raspberry Pi), set FAIRWAY_TCP_HOST to this computer's LAN address and allow that port on your trusted local network. The Open Connect TCP protocol has no authentication or encryption; do not expose it to the internet. Keep the browser and bridge on the same computer; the web interface remains loopback-bound.

Example macOS/Linux bridge command for a standard TCP port:

```sh
FAIRWAY_TCP_PORT=921 npm run bridge
```

PowerShell equivalent: `$env:FAIRWAY_TCP_PORT="921"; npm run bridge`.

Potential connector paths include PiTrac, the Garmin R10 community connector, Rapsodo MLM2PRO's connector, and OpenSkyPlus. These are protocol-level integration paths, **not a verified hardware compatibility list**. Device firmware, vendor licenses, connector versions, and operating systems affect support. This project does not include vendor drivers. See RESEARCH.md for the verified project links and protocol assumptions.

## Physics and testing

All calculations use meters, seconds, kilograms, and radians internally. Ball-flight integration runs at 240 Hz with a midpoint method; it includes gravity, Reynolds/spin-dependent approximate drag and lift, spin axis, spin decay, wind-relative velocity, temperature, and altitude. Bounce uses surface restitution, a compliant contact-plane approximation, Coulomb impulses and spin coupling. Roll uses rotational-inertia-aware slopes, calibrated green Stimp and adjustable surface resistance. Tree trunks collide; foliage does not. Both the rendered ball and physics use a 42.67 mm diameter. Trunk contacts resolve penetration and reflect only incoming motion, so a ball can escape from a close tree lie. Neighboring-hole trunks are included through a spatial index.

This is a research-informed implementation, **not an empirically calibrated replacement for a commercial simulator**. See RESEARCH.md for equations, sources, benchmarks, and limitations. Unit/integration tests cover physics plausibility, convergence, left/right curvature, wind, putting, water/out-of-bounds, deterministic biome generation, input validation, scoring formats, persistence, TCP framing, browser acknowledgments, and local-origin filtering. Physical launch monitors and physical gamepads remain untested.

For a context-free architecture and maintenance guide, read [PROJECT_HANDOFF.md](PROJECT_HANDOFF.md). Prioritized future work and known limitations are in [TODO.md](TODO.md); [AGENTS.md](AGENTS.md) is the entry point for coding agents.
