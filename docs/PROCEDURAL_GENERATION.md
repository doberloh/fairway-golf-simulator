# How Fairway generates a course

Fully procedural generation is a good fit for this simulator. It needs constraints to create playable golf, but it does not need a catalogue of prebuilt holes. The same seed and settings reproduce the same course within this generator version. Changing the generator can change what an older seed produces.

## What is generated

1. **Plan the round.** Requested yardage determines total par within 68–72 for eighteen holes or 34–36 for nine. A seeded choice selects a valid mix of par 3s, 4s and 5s, then shuffles their order. Independently varied hole distances are scaled to the requested total. Beyond the par bounds, lengths continue scaling. Very short or long requests can therefore fall outside conventional par-specific yardage ranges, as requested.
2. **Build playing lines.** Each hole gets a continuous centerline, with seeded curvature, turn size, direction and pivot location. Par 3s are always straight; dogleg frequency still allows deliberately straight par 4/5 holes. Selected doglegs balance left and right across a course; the selected turning point is a guideline with earlier and later variation.
3. **Generate fairway edges.** Each side receives its own 4–8 control points, random locations and widths, with smooth interpolation. A shared broad taper and independent local changes produce expanding, contracting and multiple landing areas without selecting named outline templates. Width limits keep the fairway playable. Par 3s can connect the tee area to the fairway.
4. **Shape the putting surface, then cut the hole.** Every green draws a character from its seed: **rolling** (three or four broad mounds and hollows), **tiered** (one real step), **ridged** (a spine or swale across it), **crowned** (a turtleback) or **bowl** (a dish that gathers) — about a third, a fifth, a fifth, a seventh and an eighth of greens respectively — plus a drainage tilt, mostly falling toward the approach, and a couple of small rolls. Only tiered greens have a step; until GENERATOR_VERSION 33 every green was the same recipe of square-wave ridges and a tier, so nearly all of them had one. The slope slider is superlinear: at the default of 35 a green averages 1.1% with about 1.1 ft of relief; at 70, 3.5% and 3.7 ft; at 100, 7.2%, 7.2 ft and faces up to about 27%, with 7% of the surface still level enough for a cup. At the top the rolls grow broader as well as taller and a tier's face widens with its step, so a severe green is steep but never a wall, and the tilt stops growing past about 70 so the difficulty is in the contours. **False fronts** (a share of greens, default 20%) drop the front of the green toward the fairway by about 1-1.6 ft at the default; they are part of the putting surface, beyond where front hole locations are cut. **Raised greens** (25%) and **punchbowl greens** (10%) lift or sink the green and its collar as one piece, 3-5 ft up or 2-3.5 ft down, with banks beyond the collar; the putting surface is unchanged by either. The contour is anchored at the green's **centre**, not at the cup, because the cup moves and the ground does not.

   The cup is then chosen from that contour by `choosePin`, scored on the slope under it and the green left around it. `pinDay` — Thursday through Sunday — sets a target slope and a hard cap; difficulty is slope, while distance to the edge is a safety floor of 3 m rather than a second dial. Bands rotate front, middle, back by hole number starting at the front. Pin choice draws from its own seeded stream so it cannot shift anything else on the course, and nothing else on the course is placed relative to it.
5. **Sculpt bunkers and hazards.** Seeded sizes, proportions and continuous boundary waves generate green and bunker outlines. Water uses sampled curving banks with changing widths, shaped relative to the nearby fairway edge. A clearance buffer protects turf; identical bank profiles drive the rendered water, terrain basin and map. Bunker count, position, distance from the fringe and placement inside fairways follow the studio settings. This is a constrained mathematical shape generator, not an unrestricted landscape-design AI.
6. **Site three tee sets.** Blue sets the reference route length; white and red move forward by independently varied amounts and shift to either side of the playing line. All three are always built and the golfer chooses during play or round setup. The pads are SITED rather than placed: a hole commits to a rough tee area before any terrain exists, and once the land is there each pad is scored over candidate positions on how flat the ground is, how little it has to be cut or filled, and how far it sits out of line with every tee already placed on that hole. The last of those is why a tee is rarely directly in front of another one -- across 945 tees, pairs sitting in line fell from 110 to 10 when the stagger began scoring against all of them rather than only the nearest. Each pad is 7.2 m long by 6 m wide with a rounded apron, its shoulder falling away 35% faster directly ahead than to the sides or behind so it does not build a lip in front of the shot; the two markers stand 6 inches in from each edge, and the hole-and-yardage sign stands off to the right of the blue tee. Facing is computed AFTER siting -- a tee squared to the shot from where it used to be is squared to nothing.
7. **Route the whole course.** The chosen footprint provides a seed-varied guiding curve: an organic sweep, elongated loop, crescent, winding ribbon, original square, figure eight, butterfly, clover, spiral, horseshoe, rounded triangle, diamond, coastal sweep or island chain. The dropdown includes small selectable SVG previews. These guide overall land use rather than prescribing individual hole shapes. Seeded candidate positions and bearings place holes near the preceding green. Corridor clearance checks reject overlaps; distance from the guiding curve, progression, compactness and walking distance guide the choice. A fallback expands the footprint if a tight placement fails. This remains a heuristic, not a certified golf-course safety or architecture model.
8. **Build one landscape.** Regional landform rules create ridges, dunes, channels and biome vegetation around the routed holes. One connected mesh carries all playing surfaces. Extra detail around greens, bunkers, pond beds/shorelines and flowing channels joins the wider terrain without cracks. The distant landscape shares the detailed ground’s perimeter vertices, eliminating exposed rectangular tile edges. Trees grow around the routed corridors, giving the course an irregular outer woodland boundary. Ball contact, shading, hazard boundaries and maps consume the same course data.

## Why keep rules?

Unconstrained randomness readily creates impassable fairways, overlapping holes, flooded tees and unplayable greens. Rules constrain those outcomes while the seed continuously varies the geometry. The remaining mathematical choices—smooth curves, bounded widths, basin excavation and terrain functions—are reusable building blocks, not a finite list of complete holes.

The current approach creates much more variety than selecting a few named shapes, but it cannot guarantee that every random course has the strategic quality of a human-designed course. Further improvement should evaluate generated routes for shot choices, forced carries, recovery areas and walking distances, rather than reintroducing a fixed hole catalogue.

## Maps and green reading

Hole length is bounded by par rather than scaled to fit: each par has a researched minimum, typical and maximum, and the course's requested length is distributed across the holes in proportion to the room each has left inside its own band. A total the bands cannot reach is clamped and reported rather than met by stretching holes past what may be called that par. The par mix is weighted toward a fifth of holes at par 3 and a fifth at par 5, and par order is split evenly across the nines before being settled against a score that penalises back-to-back short or long holes. A hole's line, length and tees are computed from the seed alone, so the full scorecard -- par and all three tee yardages -- is available before any terrain exists.

Hole maps now fit the complete generated fairway, hazards, green, tees and current ball, including wide doglegs. Drawing and click-to-aim share an invertible projection with the same left/right orientation as the playing view. Replay maps follow the recorded shot’s hole. The surround beyond the hole is the biome's own rough; `mapWater` is the colour beyond the generated LAND and belongs to the full-course map, where a terrain tile covers it. The canvas is sized to the display's pixel ratio, and the green's contour tile is baked at a higher resolution when the map frames the green, because that is the only view that magnifies it.

The three green-reading layers are switched from three icon buttons in the map header, separately or together:

- A 1.5 m grid colored by actual gradient: blue under 1%, green 1–2%, yellow 2–3%, orange 3–5%, red above 5%.
- Animated markers starting at grid vertices, following the changing downhill gradient and moving faster on steeper ground.
- A relative-elevation heat map, blue low to red high, with contour lines every 10 cm. Its triangles match the terrain and leave the cup opening clear.

These tools reveal the same ground heights used for putting. The heat map shows relative elevation; grid colors show slope steepness.

Once the ball is on the putting surface the map reframes on the green alone and paints that same contour field from directly above, contours included. Its bounds are sampled from the green outline rather than assumed circular, because greens are stretched by `greenAspect` and wobble with the hole seed. Its colour range is taken from the putting surface alone: measured against the surrounding ground, a green in a hollow scales against the bank behind it and renders as one flat colour. The tile is cached per green, and nothing is drawn when the green has no relief at all — which is the case on the driving range, where green reading is disabled outright.

## Water fitting, green surrounds and housing

Green-to-surround transitions extend 40–100 metres according to the height difference, rather than blending abruptly into distant ridges. The modifier bounds include the full transition support. This softens shoulders without changing the green difficulty control.

The ground is settled to a level shelf under each pond rather than hunting for a site that happens to be flat. Each pond reads a target elevation off the base landform around its rim and eases the surrounding ground toward it; green and tee shaping is applied afterwards and still wins, so a pond can never flatten a green complex. The shelf margin scales with the pond, so shrinking a pond to fit steep ground actually reduces the footprint it is judged over. A tee pad is a 6 by 9 metre rectangle with rounded corners, squared to the same aim the markers are -- as far down the hole as a straight line from that tee stays in the corridor, which is the dogleg corner where there is one and the landing area where there is not -- with a mown collar at 1.45 times its size. Pads prefer to stagger across the hole rather than sit in line, since a tee directly in front is a tee you have to look over. The pad -- the piece of ground -- is separate from the tee marker that stands on it, so a marker can carry a pad of its own size and position or none at all; at 6 by 9 metres three pads fit down a par three at honest spacing, so short holes use three ordinary tees and the levelling rule gives them a stepped form. Tee earthworks reach about half as far as they did and fade out before reaching a green, which previously they overwrote. The ground behind the start of mown turf keeps a quarter of the relief the surrounding country has, rather than being flattened to the corridor's base level, so a tee has real ground to be cut into instead of a saucer to be perched in. That holds whatever the elevation setting says -- elevation raises the corridor from there, and zero is a baseline rather than a promise of dead level. The driving range is exempt and stays perfectly flat, because any tilt there is a variable the player did not set. Each tee pad is sited rather than placed: once the land exists, a pad tries forty-odd nearby positions and takes the one the ground already suits, scoring flat ground, a clear shot line, sitting above the tee in front of it and a sensible angle to the fairway, and refusing any site that puts the pad on a green, in water, in sand or on another hole. It may move freely across the hole and up to 20 m along it, which keeps the hole's ponds, bunkers and fairway start where they were; yardage is recomputed from where the tee ends up. Ground falls away from a pad over a short rounded shoulder rather than a long ramp, so the disturbed area no longer grows with how far the tee was raised. A tee complex is levelled as one thing rather than as three independent pads. The three natural heights are pulled toward their mean and then ordered, so the back tee always sits at or above the one in front of it -- previously each pad read its own height off the ground and any hole that climbed off the tee inverted them, on 34% of holes. The step is scaled by the spread the ground already has, so flat land keeps flat tees and the driving range's three side-by-side mats stay identical. The whole complex is then lifted until the tee shot clears the ground in front of it, sampled along the centreline out to three quarters of the way to the landing area and capped at 3.5 m: the tee is raised rather than the hillside shaved, so the terrain the hole was generated around is untouched. Tee shots blocked by more than a metre fell from 23% to 3% off the back tee. Pads read their height from the shaped field rather than the raw landform, and ramp off it over a distance that grows with the drop, so a tee on a hillside becomes a terrace instead of a pad with a wall around it. The flattened ground is an oval matching the painted tee and reaches the whole mown collar, so the collar is flat rather than tilting on the ramp; it was previously a box, which left flat corners jutting past the paint and broke the shading along a rectangle nothing on screen agreed with. `blindTees` is the share of holes allowed to keep a blind tee shot rather than being raised to clear it, drawn per hole and defaulting to none; only holes the land makes blind can be chosen. Each pad carries a mown collar about 1.7 times its own size, classified as semi-rough and claimed only where the ground is otherwise rough, so a fairway, green or bunker beside a tee keeps its own surface. Pad and collar are sized from one pair of exported constants that both the lie classifier and the ground shader read, so the painted surface and the played lie cannot drift apart. Rim elevations on the finished terrain typically vary by a few centimetres. The remaining rim sampling, shrinking and removal stay as a safety net for sites that other features distort. The flat water level sits below the lowest sampled rim and outer-bank terrain rather than following the height at the pond centre, and the selected minimum/maximum depth still measures from the water surface to the excavated floor.

Ponds are generated against the fairway edge with a gap that may be negative, so water can sit well out in the rough, come right up to the playing corridor, or bite into it. About one pond in six reaches past the fairway edge, by a median of 5.7 m and at most 13 m, and the fairway is mown around the bite. None crosses: the inward reach is bounded at 0.70 of the corridor half width and measured from the semi-rough edge, so the near shore cannot arrive at the centreline for any value the generator can produce. Because a pond's banks are anchored outside the fairway edge, enlarging one moves it away from play rather than into it.

River and creek controls request a bounded number of course-wide meandering corridors. Tees, greens and fitted ponds have protected clearance areas; accepted paths are rounded, given variable widths and terrain-fitted levels, and indexed spatially. That same path drives bed carving, water surfaces, water lies and map lines. Par threes are not given a fairway at all: the ball is played through the air to the green, so a full corridor down the hole is turf nobody uses and it swallows the tee complex. They get an approach instead, beginning at `mowStart` rather than `fairwayStart` — never wider than the green it fronts and never covering more than the last 45% of the hole — and every bunker on a par three is greenside, because there is no fairway to bunker. `fairwayStart` still anchors bunker and pond placement on every hole, so par threes keep their hazards. Rivers and creeks do not cross fairways, greens or other bodies of water. Corridors and greens are raised in the drainage model so routes go between the holes -- and the corridor ridge keeps rising inward rather than levelling off at the corridor edge, because a constant offset preserves the gradient underneath it and simply becomes a plateau that water runs through, and the finished curve is then pushed clear of anything it must not touch and re-smoothed, because meander and corner cutting are applied after the route is chosen and would otherwise undo it. Measured over 24 courses: no channel station inside a fairway, a minimum of 11 m past any green edge, and a minimum of 26 m from the edge of any pond or lake. Creek depths are 60% of river depth, and no bridges are generated yet. A channel runs only as far as it can stay within its cut budget, and fades out where it is trimmed.

Optional homes are sampled along both sides of the fairways with occurrence and setback controls. Sites must be dry rough, gently sloping, spaced from other homes and clear of tee/green complexes. Houses face their corridor and trees are excluded from their footprints. They currently are decorative scenery without collision rules.

For the implementation map and extension checklist, see PROJECT_HANDOFF.md. TODO.md covers hydrology, routing quality, building collisions, performance and other future improvements.

## What a biome decides

The redwood grove's plants are GROWN rather than imported. `tools/grow.mjs` composes every one of them -- redwood, douglas fir, hemlock, red cedar, tanoak, seedlings, sword fern, salal, sorrel, plus nurse logs, stumps and boulders -- from a small geometry library, with shapes and colours measured from 315 photographs of real redwood forest. Each is a whole tree carrying its own fluted, buttressed trunk, so nothing is borrowed and nothing is drawn underneath. This replaced an earlier approach that drew a tapered cylinder and balanced a borrowed conifer crown on top, which existed only because no imported conifer is anything but conical to the ground and reads as a Christmas tree at redwood scale.

Every tree draws at full detail. A two-level swap by camera distance was built here and removed: it was justified by arithmetic and then by a benchmark that was reading the display's vsync interval rather than the cost of the geometry. Measured honestly, a grove draws 33.6 M vertices a frame and still holds the refresh cap, while the swap made nearly every visible tree the thinned twin -- a forest that looked dead, and a boundary that popped.

Elsewhere a tall conifer is still an imported model. The crown's width is stated as a fraction of the tree's own height with the model's native width divided out, so every crown in a grove is the width asked for whichever model it came from, and the crown takes whatever height the trunk leaves, so a tree is exactly as tall as its stated height. A trunk is drawn at the radius physics collides with, so a trunk you can see is a trunk you hit. Tree height is a biome field: 13 to 29 metres ordinarily, 42 to 116 -- 380 feet -- in a redwood grove.

**A bunker sits wholly on its own hole's ground** (generator 34), as a pond already must. Once the holes are placed, any bunker whose sand or lip (1.15 times its outline, the excavation's reach) touches ground another hole owns is dropped. The ground is painted with the owning hole's hazards only, so such a bunker used to be dug out and then grassed over by its neighbour's rough. About one bunker in twenty went this way; it is dropped rather than moved, and no random draw is taken, so nothing else on the course shifts.

A green's outline is its nominal size times one plus three harmonics -- a two-lobed wave, a three-lobed one and a five -- and a bunker's is the same idea with two. `greenShape` and `bunkerShape` raise the amplitude of those harmonics and, as they rise, pull them toward equal so that no single wave takes over the outline. Simply scaling the drawn mix was tried first and rejected: it preserved which harmonic dominated, and since the three-lobed wave leads on most greens, turning the slider up produced a clean three-lobed flower -- which, stretched by a green's aspect, is two lobes and a shaft rather than anything golf-like. The scaling happens at generation, so the values stored on the hole and the bunker are the ones both the geometry and the ground shader read; there is no second copy of the arithmetic to drift. A cap on total amplitude keeps a radius positive, so an outline can pinch to a waist but never fold through itself. Green-side bunkers are fitted to the green AFTER their outline is scaled, so a raggeder bunker still leaves the gap it is told to.

Where planting is ALLOWED is a separate question from how densely it packs, and it is decided by four rules that run in order.

Nothing stands on mown turf, ever. Nothing stands where a tee shot would pass through it: each tee's own nominal shot is traced and anything whose trunk spans the ball's height at that distance is refused, judged along the line the shot actually plays rather than the direction the tee pad faces. Nothing stands inside the **tee fan**, the combined cone of all three tees' wedges -- judged as one shape because a tree can miss the back tee's own line and still sit squarely in the view from it, the three tees being staggered and sometimes ninety metres apart across a hole. The fan ignores height, since a tree the ball flies over still hides where the ball is going.

The fourth rule is an edge rather than a wall. Planting used to stop dead at a fixed distance outside a hole's corridor, and a cliff produces two artefacts at once: a bare moat, and a pile-up just outside it where every refused candidate was pushed. Acceptance now climbs from nothing at a floor to certainty a little further out, so candidates near the edge are sometimes kept. **Near a tee the floor is a multiple of that hole's own corridor half-width, not a number of metres** -- the distance it is compared against grows with the fairway, so a fixed floor held at the default width and collapsed at the top of the range.

Greens are treated asymmetrically on purpose. The arc facing back down the fairway keeps its clearance whatever `greenTrees` says, because an approach has to have somewhere to land; the slider moves only the back and the flanks. The approach direction is taken from the middle of the hole seventy metres short of the green, so a dogleg's approach is where the shot really comes from.

A hole may also be given a **specimen**: a feature tree or a cluster of stones standing in its own short grass, controlled by `fairwayFeature`. This is the one obstruction allowed on the line of play -- an oak in the middle of a fairway is the hole, not a fault -- and what makes it fair is the open grass down one side rather than an empty centre.

**Boulders are generation output, not decoration.** They used to be placed by the renderer from a stream the generator never saw, so the world did not know where they were and nothing could collide with one. They carry their reach and crown height now, and the renderer reads them.

Trees leave room for each other where a biome asks them to (`crownShare`): two crowns may not share more than that fraction of their combined radii, and trunks may never intersect. It is on for the redwood grove and off everywhere else, since no other biome has a tree wide enough for it to matter. A plant entry may carry a height scale as its third number, which is how a biome gets a mid-storey under its canopy.

A biome may also ask for **deadfall** -- fallen logs, stumps and mossy boulders on the forest floor. It is a count, and zero means a clean floor. Roughly three quarters of it is placed around an existing trunk rather than at a uniform random point, because timber falls where timber grows and an even scatter reads as litter on a lawn. It is decoration: nothing collides with it.


A biome is one record in `src/biomes.js` holding 45 fields: its palette and light, the shape and strength of the hills between corridors, whether it has a sea and whether that sea is all round it or along one edge, its plant mix and tree density, how much rock and grass is scattered and how large, the colour of a stream bank and of what a shot throws up, and a handful of flags the ground shader reads by name. Defaults cover every field, so a biome states only what makes it different.

## Large lakes and downhill streams

Large lakes are additional continuous procedural bank profiles, requested with count and typical-diameter sliders. Candidate search favors low, gentler open ground outside the playing corridors. They share the pond basin/reflection/map pipeline. A lake that extends across nearest-hole tiles retains its own surface and GPU ownership; the per-hole atlas limit remains four ponds/lakes. Available space may reduce the requested count.

Channels are not drawn across the map; they are read off the land's own drainage. The whole course is solved once on a 10 m grid — depressions flooded to their spill height by a priority flood, each cell pointed at its steepest lower neighbour, and flow accumulated from the top down — and every watercourse is a walk down those flow directions. That construction is what keeps a channel from crossing itself: every step is strictly lower than the last. Specs are taken strongest first, so the river claims the largest catchment and creeks claim what drains into it, which makes a creek a tributary rather than an independent squiggle. Tees, greens, fitted ponds and bunkers are RAISED in the working height field rather than steered around, so water flows past them the way it flows past a hill and the route stays a pure descent. Meander is then applied as a bounded lateral offset, tapered to nothing at both ends, never as a rotation of the heading — a rotation integrates, and a constant bend is a circle. Corner cutting and a curvature limit hold every bend wider than the channel itself, which keeps the bank from folding through itself where a channel crosses a fairway. Where a course leaves no route clear of a bunker, the channel washes the crossed bunker out rather than leaving water standing in sand, and bunker count is already an upper target.

Water elevations stay below sampled terrain and descend continuously, with longitudinal grades limited to 0.015–9%. The upper figure is the grade a channel is allowed to shed, not one it seeks: a river runs at one or two per cent and a mountain creek at ten or more, and holding the old 2.5% against a route that follows a real valley forced a 6.9 m median excavation wherever the valley fell faster than the water was permitted to. Because levels only descend, one hollow near the head pins every station after it, and any later ridge would have to be trenched through to hold that level; rather than cut a gorge, the profile keeps the longest run whose bed stays within `MAX_CUT` of the ground beside it and trims the rest. A trimmed end is a headwater or a sink, so the channel closes down to nothing over `FADE` instead of ending at a blunt face, and a candidate whose surviving run is too short is rejected outright — on very hilly courses that can mean fewer channels than requested. A channel's downstream end is classified as it is traced: off the edge of the map, the sea, another channel it has joined, or a sink. A depression only survives as a sink above `SINK_FILL_AREA` (15 000 m²); below that it is flooded away as noise. A channel reaching a sink crosses it to the bottom and ends in a real pond, created through the same fitting every other pond uses so it carries the same cut bank, shore band, mown collar, map outline and overlap rules. The stations that fall in the water are dropped and the last 60 m of channel is ramped down to the pond's level, so the two surfaces meet at the shore. Graded shoulders carve the remaining channel through intervening ground while protecting tees, greens and pond banks. This produces consistent downstream levels without claiming catchment hydrology or connected tributaries. Banks step through a wet margin and damp earth and then fade into the surrounding cut, each band scaled to the channel width and to the surface being crossed: mown turf is maintained close to the water, while unmaintained ground keeps a margin about three times wider. The change between the two is gradual, beginning roughly 70 m before a river reaches a fairway, so the bank tapers rather than stepping. Across the crossing itself the turf boundaries blend and the mowing stripes fade, which is the one place the course does not draw a crisp edge; it affects appearance only, and a ball's lie is still decided at the true boundary. The bank stays earth-toned throughout, and grass is kept off it, so a channel does not trace a coloured ribbon across the ground it crosses. River/creek surfaces share the pond reflector, approximated at the nearest station elevation, and thin out at the waterline.

## Obstructions, and the rule every body obeys

Scatter rocks and planted trees are both refused where they would stand in a tee
shot (`blocksLaunch`, the launch cone from each tee along the line the shot
actually takes) or in the combined view from all three tees (`inTeeFan`). Both
tests are given the body's own width -- a trunk's girth, a stone's `reach` -- and
the stone's real crown (`top`), not a stand-in for either. A rock that fails
either test is discarded rather than moved.

A FEATURE is the exception and is meant to be: a specimen tree, a desert cactus
or a cluster of stones placed in the fairway on purpose from 95 m out. It obeys
`FEATURE_STANDOFF` on the near stretch of a tee shot instead, leaves `FEATURE_GAP`
of open grass down one side, and keeps clear of the green. A biome whose species
are all ground cover -- links -- gets stones rather than a shrub grown to canopy
height.

## Tee complexes

Each tee is sited by a score over candidate ground: flatness first, then the
order of the tees, then how much it can see, then how far out of line it sits
with **every** tee already placed. The pad is 6 by 7.2 m with a mown collar
1.45 times that, and its plateau reaches the collar's edge so the whole mown
surface is flat. The shoulder that falls from the plateau to natural ground is
a fixed reach with whatever steepness the drop makes -- and 35% shorter
directly ahead than to the sides or behind, because the bank that makes a tee
read as built when you see it from the side is a slope between you and the
fairway when you stand on it.

Markers sit six inches in from each edge of the pad, and the hole sign stands
beside the blue tee on the player's right.

A tee is raised only after siting has failed to find a view, and never by more
than `TEE_LIFT_CAP`. Blind tee shots that remain are the landscape at the
landing area rather than the tee: measured, every one of them is caused by
ground 96 to 190 m out.
