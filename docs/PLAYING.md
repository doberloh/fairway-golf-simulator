# Playing Fairway

Everything a player can see, set or press. Setting up -- starting the server,
playing on a phone, connecting a launch monitor, and what to do when something
will not cooperate -- is in the README beside the game in the download.

This file ships in the download beside that README. It names other documents
rather than linking to them; those live in the repository and in the source
archive, where INSTALLATION.md covers building Fairway yourself.

## Play

Click the course or hole map to set an aim point, or enter **Aim distance (yd)**. **Aim at pin** targets the cup. The **‹ ›** arrows beside the aim readout turn the line half a degree a click; hold one to sweep, slowly at first and faster the longer you hold. The flag and aim markers over the course show the distance to the pin and to the aim point, taking turns: the pin's distance is the one you see, and moving the aim swaps it for the aim point's until a second after you stop. On the green, when the cup is off the screen, the flag marker waits at the screen's edge with a small arrow on its ring pointing toward the cup; the flag in it stays upright. A manually selected aim distance stays fixed when changing club; arrow/controller aiming rotates it around the current lie. The initial target follows the middle of the fairway for the selected club’s stock carry, including through doglegs; near the end of the route or on the green it targets the pin. Thick animated dashes and small aiming markers travel from the ball toward the target in the scene and on the map; the shot tracer is thicker too. Choose a club and swing with the **Swing Meter** (below): **Space** three times, or the **Swing** button. ← → turn the aim and ↑ ↓ move the aim point further and shorter; **S**, **W** and **X** choose the curve, the height and the spin; **A** puts the aim back where the turn started it; Q/E or **I/K** change clubs; C toggles the overview; **G** flies the camera to the aim point, then to the green, then back to the ball; Enter finishes an animation; **H** hides everything drawn over the course, and H or Escape brings it back; **Tab** lists every key. On a standard gamepad, the left stick aims, A/Cross swings, the D-pad sets the curve (← →) and height (↑ ↓), X/Square adds spin, shoulder buttons change clubs, Y/Triangle changes camera, and B/Circle skips flight or closes a panel. Press a controller button once so the browser can discover it. Actual controller hardware has not been tested in this environment.

Course studio offers Pacific Northwest, Desert, Mountain, Links, Midwest, Island, Giant Redwood, Autumn and Haunted Hollow landscapes. Giant Redwood is the moody one: ancient bare-trunked redwoods and douglas fir lining the fairways with fern underbrush, dark lush greens, and a sun low enough all day to rake light between the trunks and hang haze in the gaps. Every plant in it is generated rather than imported: coast redwoods running to 380 feet on fluted, buttressed trunks that stay bare for two thirds of their height, douglas fir and hemlock between them, a western red cedar and tanoak mid-storey, conifer seedlings, and a floor of sword fern, salal and redwood sorrel among nurse logs, stumps and moss-covered boulders. Their shapes and colours were measured from 315 photographs of real redwood forest. **Haunted Hollow** is the Halloween one, and it is full fantasy: purple fairways and violet rough leading to slime-green greens, ash-grey bunkers and dark green water, under gnarled dead oaks, tall pale snags and bare willows. Giant pumpkins -- some taller than you -- grow in patches in the rough, with toadstools the size of a person round the trunks; most pumpkins are carved, and their faces light up and flicker as the sun goes down. Ghosts drift on their rounds over every hole. As the light goes, a huge orange harvest moon rises low in the east, flocks of bats wheel round the tallest dead trees, and will-o'-the-wisps -- small green lights -- drift over the ponds and settle in the hollows, going out now and then. It has the heaviest fog of any landscape and a low orange sun under a mauve sky, and is at its best from Golden hour on (Sim time). The air is cold -- 9 °C -- so the ball carries a touch shorter than on a warm course. **The full scorecard is there before you build anything.** Course length, par and every tee's yardage sit in one box in the menu and in Course studio: move the length slider and the card redraws -- par per hole, out, in and total, blue, white and red. These are the yardages the course will actually have, not an estimate, because the card and the builder work the hole out the same way from the same seed. Hole lengths are held to real ranges for their par, so a par 5 cannot come out at 723 yards and starve the par 4s around it to pay for itself; ask for a length the pars cannot reach and the card tells you what it could reach instead. Par 3s and par 5s are spread across both nines rather than stacked on one.

Every seed creates an entire 9- or 18-hole course together in one shared landscape. Moving to the next hole keeps that landscape loaded. Water frequency, tree density, elevation, fairway width, green fringe width (0–6 m), semi-rough width (0–15 m), wind speed, and wind direction are adjustable. The ground is one continuous mesh with crisp fairway, semi-rough, fringe, green and sand boundaries and mowing stripes. Where a creek or river runs through a playing surface those turf edges soften and the mowing stripes fade into the bank, so the crossing reads as one gradient; this is the only place the boundaries are not crisp, and it changes appearance only — your lie is still decided at the true edge. **Graphics quality has four tiers**, and they are device-local -- a tier belongs to the machine you play on and never to the course, so two players on different settings play the identical hole. Low draws below native resolution and upscales, for a laptop with no graphics card; Ultra carries the largest shadows, reaching furthest, with bloom, the biggest reflections, and a forest floor of ferns and fallen sticks round you under the trees -- decoration only, ankle high, and nothing a ball can hit. **Automatic resolution** (Graphics, under the frame rate cap, on by default at every tier) draws fewer pixels when frames run slow -- a step at a time, down to half the tier's own -- and takes them back once there is room, trying one step sharper after a few seconds of smooth play and waiting longer each time a try does not hold, so it settles rather than flickers. It aims for 60 frames a second, or your cap if that is lower, never for a faster display's full rate, and it never draws sharper than the tier. The panel says what it is drawing at right now. Switch it off if you would rather the picture stayed exactly as sharp as the tier and the frame rate went where it goes. **Text size** (Graphics, just below) makes everything on screen larger or smaller — panels, numbers, menus — from 75% to 200%, for a projector or any screen you stand back from; the course itself is drawn exactly as sharp at any size. **Larger flags far away** (just below, off by default) grows the flagsticks with distance so the hole is always easy to find; off, they are to size from everywhere. **Size text automatically** (on by default) keeps it at 100% at a desk, and in simulator bay mode works it out from your screen size, how far back you stand and how many pixels your browser is across, so text looks at least as large from where you stand as it does on a laptop — on the default bay with a 1080p projector that is still 100%, since text there already looks bigger than on a laptop; it rises with a 4K projector or a longer stance. A size is never larger than the screen can hold — 150% on a 1080p screen, 200% on a 2560-wide or 4K one — and the panel says so when it holds one back. Ground cues -- relief shading, slope tint, contours and mowing stripes -- are switches rather than tiers, because measured they cost nothing: nobody should drop to Low to turn a look off.

**The wind dial reads against the shot in front of you.** The arrow lies flat on the ground rather than printed on the glass, and it turns as you turn: straight up is wind going away from you, right is a crosswind running left to right. Leaves and dust drift past on the same wind, each one weaving on its own and trailing a small tail.

**Cartoon is the single art style**, with sculpted foliage, rough grass that leans downwind with gusts travelling across it (the semi-rough is mown turf and carries no blades — its cut height is the information), golden Links prairie, conifer pine-straw patches, drifting clouds and reflective water with foam at every edge, each in its own way: lacy lapping round ponds, a wider, livelier wash on lakes, surf running up and draining back on the sea, lace drifting downstream along river banks, and white flecks carried along creeks -- and the water of a river or creek flows the way the channel runs, round every bend, plus sparks where the ripples catch the sun. Trees bend and grass ripples as gusts cross the course; every surface runs in patches of lighter, darker, drier and lusher turf; the ground darkens softly at the foot of trunks and boulders and under a forest; and far land fades toward the colour of the sky. The ground past the course runs out to the horizon without seams or streaks. Fairway edges vary independently. Course studio's **Fairway bunker occurrence** controls how often non-greenside bunkers sit inside the fairway; bunkers have excavated floors and local turf at the lip rather than a generic rough collar.

Course routing uses varied bearings and short green-to-tee transfers. **Course footprint** has 14 choices with selectable preview icons: Organic sweep, Elongated loop, Crescent, Winding ribbon, Original square, Figure eight, Butterfly, Three-leaf clover, Spiral, Horseshoe, Rounded triangle, Diamond, Coastal sweep and Island chain. These are guiding landscape curves, with seed-varied proportions and collision-checked placement; individual holes remain fully procedural. Woodland follows the routed corridors, and the detailed ground joins the distant landscape without an exposed rectangular tile edge. **Landscape character** controls inter-hole mountains, dunes, or island channels. **Space between hole corridors** controls routing clearance. **Dogleg frequency**, **Maximum dogleg turn**, and **Dogleg turning point** shape par 4/5 fairways. Par 3 centerlines are always straight, with restrained width changes. Left/right par 4/5 turns are balanced, and each pivot varies up to 22 percentage points earlier or later than the selected point. **Bunkers per hole** is an upper target; **Greenside bunker gap from fringe** allows close defensive placement, including zero gap. **Minimum/maximum water hazard depth** selects each lake's deepest point, with shallow banks and a local water elevation. Pond outlines have independently changing widths and curving banks that follow the neighboring fairway/semi-rough edge. Maps, water meshes, sand margins and collision basins share those bank profiles. Coastal channel floors respect the maximum too. These settings apply when you press **Generate & play**, rebuilding the whole landscape and starting a fresh round.

Elevation now has a much larger effect on playable corridors, with tens of metres of height change at high settings. Fairways have no finite outline families: each side uses 4–8 independently generated control points with smooth transitions, a variable broad taper, and local narrowing/widening. Green sizes, proportions and outlines, bunker outlines, fairway starts and hazard locations vary continuously. The width slider is a guideline. Par 3 fairways sometimes connect directly to their tee area. See how procedural generation works (`PROCEDURAL_GENERATION.md`). Airborne leaves or light dust drift in the course-wide wind direction. Density increases gently with wind, is capped at 76 nearby particles, and is zero in calm conditions. Striking the ball adds a brief flash/ring and turf or sand spray, with a much lighter putting effect. These effects are visual and do not alter ball contact. Cartoon turf combines subtle procedural grain with wind-driven grass and canopy geometry, animated flags and slowly moving clouds.

Clouds drift on the course's own wind, thinning out as they leave and thickening as they arrive rather than appearing and vanishing whole. The shadow a cloud throws fades with it.

**Hole flyover** on the camera toolbar circles the whole hole from above — the same camera that orbits the showcase hole behind the main menu, at one and a half times its speed, one lap of about 56 seconds. It keeps well clear of ridges and treetops on any terrain. It returns to your ball and restores your green-reading overlays; the button, Escape, another camera or any panel stops it early.

**Typical fairway width** runs from 20 to 92 m — the top of that range is a hundred yards, which is links territory rather than a normal fairway. It is a guideline rather than a constant: each side of the corridor is generated independently, so a hole widens into its landing areas and pinches at the approach.

**Blind tee shots** sets how often a hole is allowed to keep one. A tee is normally raised until the shot clears whatever stands in front of it; this is the share of holes left alone instead. Only holes the land actually makes blind can be chosen, so the real rate tops out near one in five however high it goes. The default is none.

**Elevation severity** at zero is a baseline rather than a promise of dead level: the ground behind a tee keeps its own relief so a tee has real ground to be cut into, and elevation raises the playing corridor from there. The driving range is the exception and stays perfectly flat, because any tilt there is a variable you did not set, quietly added to every carry.

**Typical pond size** scales both pond dimensions from 40–300 m. Because pond banks are anchored outside the fairway edge, a larger pond grows away from play rather than into it. **Large lakes** requests 0–3 larger water bodies, with a **Typical lake diameter** of 60–460 m. Ordinary ponds are substantial too, typically spanning around 90 m; lakes are for genuinely large water. At the top of the lake range, open ground runs out and fewer lakes are placed than requested. Placement favors open, gentler ground and may produce fewer lakes where there is insufficient room. They use the existing minimum/maximum water depth. River and creek banks blend biome-specific damp earth, grass and stone colors into the surrounding turf. Channels descend through smooth terrain cutouts and share the ponds’ reflective water. Water is translucent and thins out at a channel’s edge instead of stopping against the bank; pond, lake and ocean edges do not thin. Every pond, lake and channel is the same surface, and reflects its own surroundings from a small cubemap taken where that body sits when the course is built. There is no single mirror handed between bodies any more, so water never changes character as you walk past it.

Tees are sited rather than placed. Each pad tries a few dozen nearby positions once the land exists and takes the one the ground already suits — flat, with a clear view, sitting above the tee in front of it, and never on a green, in water, in sand or on another hole. That is where the variety comes from: a back tee on a rise, a forward tee tucked in beside the fairway, tees at genuinely different heights. A pad is a 6 by 9 m rectangle with rounded corners, squared to as far down the hole as a straight line from that tee stays inside the corridor — the dogleg corner where there is one, the landing area where there is not. Short holes get three ordinary tees stepping down, not one long shared box.

Rivers and creeks are read off the land's own drainage rather than drawn across it. The whole course is solved once — depressions flooded to their spill height, every cell pointed at its steepest lower neighbour, and the drainage accumulated — so a creek is a tributary of the river rather than an independent squiggle. Water never crosses a fairway, a green or another body of water, and a channel that reaches a hollow ends in a pond built like any other.

Every biome now mixes several plant forms: conifers/alder/ferns; desert trees/cacti/rosettes; conifers/aspen; grasses/heath/gorse; parkland broadleaf trees; palms/hala/coastal shrubs; or mixed fall woodland. See landscape research and aerial references (`LANDSCAPE_RESEARCH.md`) for the sources and design decisions.

**Course length** sets target blue-tee yardage, with a live total par and par-3/4/5 breakdown. Total par stays at 68–72 for 18 holes and 34–36 for nine; at the extremes, distance continues changing while par stays within those limits. The seed randomizes the par mix and hole order. The target supports 990–4,230 yards for nine holes and 1,980–8,460 for eighteen. Individual rounded hole yardages can make the total differ by a few yards. All courses include **Blue, White and Red tees**, with actual pads, markers set six inches in from each edge, a hole sign standing to the right of the blue tee, independently varied forward positions, and lateral offsets chosen so that no tee sits directly in front of another one -- a tee you have to look over is a tee in the wrong place. Choose **Playing tee** in the bottom controls or round setup; tee selection is separate from generation. Changing it before the first stroke moves you to that tee; after a stroke, the preference applies on the next hole. White tees average roughly 9% shorter and red roughly 18%. Scorecards and CSV exports include all three yardage rows.

**Green slope & difficulty** ranges from level greens at 0% to genuinely severe ones at 100%. Each green has a character of its own — broad rolls, a tier, a ridge or swale, a crowned turtleback, or a bowl — so no two play alike. The scale is deliberately uneven: the default of 35% gives gentle greens with about a foot of relief, 70% about 3.7 ft, and 100% about 7 ft with faces well past twenty per cent, beyond most championship greens. Even at the top, part of every green stays flat enough to cut a hole on. Past 75% the slider turns red as you drag, and when you let go a bubble pops out beside it warning that these greens are harder than almost any real course (it goes when you click anywhere, after a few seconds, or when you bring the slider back under 75%; the red stays); 90% is as severe as the old 100%, and 100% is extreme. **Raised greens**, **Punchbowl greens** and **False fronts** (in the course studio under *Fairways & greens*) set the share of greens built each way: raised greens stand on a pedestal with banks falling away, so a miss runs off and the chip back climbs; punchbowls sit in a hollow that feeds misses back toward the green; a false front's first few metres fall back toward the fairway, so an approach landing just short of the shelf rolls off. None of the three changes the putting surface where cups are cut. **Pin difficulty** cuts the cups for a day of a tournament: Thursday takes the flattest ground with the most room, and Friday through Sunday move to progressively more slope. No day will cut a cup where a ball cannot come to rest, or nearer than about three paces to an edge. Hole locations work front, middle, back and around again as the round goes on, and the hole card shows which you are playing. Rendering and ball contact use the same ground heights. **Green reading** is three independent tools, each a small button along the top of the course map: a slope-colored 1.5 m grid, which in the play view is squared to the direction you face at the start of each shot (one set of lines runs that way, the other straight across) and then stays put while you aim, and in the other views follows the hole; **flow**, light running along those same grid lines downhill — brighter the more of the slope runs along a line, quicker on steeper ground, gentle on the gentlest slopes and still only where the green is dead level — which works with the grid on or off; and an elevation heat map with 10 cm contour lines. Both fade out where the green is far enough away that 1.5 m squares would be a few pixels. Blue/green/yellow/orange/red grid colors indicate increasing slope. Heat map blue is lower elevation and red is higher on that green. Use any combination. They sit on the map because that is what they describe, and they can be switched at any time — you no longer open a panel to reach them. These display controls need no regeneration and remain separate from Stimp. They are off and disabled on the driving range, where the practice green is deliberately flat and every overlay would paint one featureless colour.

**The map follows the ball onto the green.** Once the ball is on the putting surface the map stops showing four hundred yards of fairway you have finished with, frames the green alone and paints the same contour field from straight above — blue low, red high, lines every 10 cm. Read from the ball, the far half of a green is a few pixels tall, which is exactly the half you are trying to judge; from overhead it is legible. It is the same height field the 3D overlay draws and the same field the putt rolls on. Step off the green and the hole map comes back.

**When you putt, the pin stays in the hole while you line up and is lifted out as the ball comes near** -- within about three metres -- the way a playing partner attends it, so it is clear before the ball arrives; a missed putt puts it back for the next. The ball is never stopped by it on a putt. **While it is in, it is solid**: chip or pitch at it from off the green and a ball that hits the flagstick bounces off it, as it would off a tree, and may drop. Flags are drawn to their real size, so from a long way out the stick can be hard to pick out; **Larger flags far away** (Graphics, off by default) draws the stick and flag larger with distance so they never shrink to nothing, and still to size up close. Only the picture changes: the ball hits the real flagstick either way. The cup stays. Once the flagstick is lifted the floating marker names the hole rather than the pin, and it no longer drifts off the edge of the screen when you turn away: it pins itself to the nearest clear edge, points back at the cup, and keeps the distance readable. It clears the shot controls and the hole map rather than hiding behind them, and it follows those panels if you drag them. The putt line rides on the green itself rather than floating above the ball, and a putter is only selected for you when the ball is actually on the putting surface -- from the fringe or a greenside bunker you get a club to chip with.

**Saved courses** is its own place on the main menu: play anything you have kept, save the landscape you are on, and move courses in and out — as a short code for one course, or as a file holding the whole library. Importing a file adds to what you have rather than replacing it, and a course you already have is recognised and skipped, so re-importing a backup does not give you two of everything. **Saved rounds** live in the round dialogue's own tab, beside the export and import that make them.

Use the player avatar or format button to create a 1–4 player, 9/18-hole local round. Stroke play keeps each golfer active until they finish the hole. Scramble rotates through teammates and lets the team select a lie; only the chosen result's penalty is counted. Match play uses singles or two best-ball teams and finishes when the lead exceeds remaining holes. Match teams must each have at least one golfer. Match play does not implement alternate-shot/foursomes or handicaps.

Putting offers three modes, set in two places that share one copy of the setting: the **Format & tees** section of the round panel, where the mode and its distances sit inline with format and tees, and *Putting options* in the tools tray during a round. Both write the same choice; the round-panel fields apply as soon as they are changed. **Hole out** requires actual cup capture, with a 107.95 mm diameter cup, a floor 101.6 mm down — the four inches the USGA requires — and a 42.67 mm ball. The rim is a surface the ball genuinely runs on rather than a rule about speed: a putt can catch the lip, ride it round — up to about two thirds of the way on a quick green — and either drop or come back out at you, and how long it rides depends on the green's Stimp, because the lip costs the ball what its own turf costs it. Once a ball is below the lip it drops: the inside of the cup cannot grip a slow ball hard enough to carry it round, so a holed putt does not circle the hole. Dead centre the cup catches a ball arriving at about 1.75 m/s. There is no gimme distance. A close putting camera activates when playing from a green; the white locator disappears within 5 m of the cup. **Dartboard** awards 1, 2, or 3 putts only after a shot stops on the current green. **Decimal** interpolates between the three adjustable distances and records two decimal places. At 2/8/16-yard thresholds, a 5-yard leave adds 1.50 putts and a 12-yard leave adds 2.50. The shot itself also counts. Both automatic modes cap at three putts outside the outer circle. Rings are visible only where they lie on the green. In scramble, players choose a candidate before its automatic putts are applied. Existing scores are not recalculated when the mode changes.

**Turf firmness** — Burnt, Firm, Normal or Soft — is not the same thing as green speed. It decides what a ball does the moment it lands: how high it bounces and whether it holds. The four settings are the USGA's own published GS3 reference bands, measured as how far a dropped ball penetrates the surface in inches, so "Firm" means what a superintendent means by it. A stock 7-iron into a green runs about 9 yards on soft turf and 16 on burnt, with the same carry. Firmness also decides how far a spinning ball can come back: a full wedge with 12,000 rpm comes back about 4 yards on Soft, 3 on Normal, 1 on Firm and half a yard on Burnt, and below about 10,000 rpm a full wedge checks rather than coming back. A steeper landing comes back sooner. Green speed and the roll settings decide what happens once it is already rolling, so a green can be quick and soft, or slow and baked hard.

**Your bag is your own.** Each golfer's clubs are kept with their profile: up to 14, each with a name you choose ("Big Dog", "54 degree"), a kind (driver, 3, 5 or 7 wood, 3-5 hybrid, 3-9 iron, pitching, gap, sand or lob wedge) and a full-swing carry (putter: its full-power roll). The kind decides how the club launches and spins; your carry decides how far, and the game works out the ball speed that carries exactly that on level ground in still air. Change it in a round from **My bag** in the tools (it opens on whoever is up, and with several golfers you can pick whose), or from **My profile → Edit bag**. A new golfer starts with the stock eight clubs; **Restore the stock bag** puts them back. The club picker lists your bag longest first, and **I** / **K** step through it. A full, clean swing on the Swing Meter carries exactly that yardage on level ground in still air; levels and settings never add distance. Wind, elevation and lie can still change actual distance. **On the Swing Meter, a shot short of a full swing keeps less spin**: a chip carries spin in proportion to its power, so it lands and releases instead of checking, while a pitch and a full swing keep nearly all of theirs (a 50 yard wedge about 6,700 rpm, as Trackman measures). **A ball landing in a bunker loses its spin to the sand**, so it stays about where it lands; only a steep face can roll it back.

**Sim drop** opens at the current lie. Click terrain or the map, enter sideways/along-hole offsets in yards, then choose **Place ball** or **Cancel**. There is no penalty. Scramble drops move the whole team from its shared lie; finish a pending team selection first. Drops can reach anywhere inside the generated course boundary. **Replay last shot** plays the recorded flight or putt without changing the ball’s current lie, player, scores, or undo history. It also works after moving to the next hole, until course regeneration or a fresh round. Replay is session-only and is cleared on reload; it uses the original recorded path rather than resimulating with changed settings. Replaying pauses automatic scorecard advancement. **Mulligan** restores the previous shot's lie, player, penalty, scores and team/match state, and takes that shot's tracer off the hole with it — the shots before it stay. It reaches back only into **the hole you are playing**: on a fresh tee there is nothing of your own to take back yet and the button says so. Up to 30 shots are retained, including across reloads. Shots made in older versions have no undo history.

**The play screen.** The **top bar** carries the hole — its number out of the round's holes, par, yardage, and the pin (front, middle or back, with the day the cups are cut for beside the label) — and a **score chip** for every player: their colour, where they stand against par, and the shot they are on or that they are in. The player who is up is lit. Tap any chip to open the scorecard. The **shot controls** run down the left: the club, the tee, the aim, the shot's curve, height and spin, the lie, the **Swing Meter** button and **Swing**. The Swing Meter's bar sits along the foot of the course, above the number tiles. At the top of them, under **Last shot**, is the **shot line**: whose shot and which club, **Skip** while a ball is up, and a chevron that hides the number tiles (leaving carry and total on the line) and shows them again; they start shown. The **number tiles** — the shot in the air, or the last one, in the numbers you pick in **Shot data** — sit in a row along the foot of the course, between the shot controls and the map. On the right: the wind at the top, the camera buttons down the edge, the course map at the foot. The course name sits in the top bar on a big screen and in free flight.

**The tracer follows a moment behind the ball**, so the line never hides the ball itself; it closes up once the ball stops.

**While a ball is in the air the screen clears**, at every size and for replays too: the shot controls, the camera buttons and the aim pad step aside until the ball settles. What stays is the top bar, the wind, the map — which follows the ball, drawing its line as it flies — the number tiles, and the **shot line**, with **Skip** on it: the shot controls shrink to that one line, at every size, and with a monitor armed so does the launch monitor window. The tiles show every number the strike decided the moment the ball leaves the club, and **carry and total count up as the ball flies**: carry stops where the ball first lands, total keeps going through the bounce and the roll. Hang time ticks the same way, and the landing angle and speed appear when it lands. Once the ball stops the shot line counts down to the next shot, and everything comes back where it was. On the Swing Meter the tiles are not shown at all: the meter stays up through the flight instead, with carry, total and apex.

**On a phone** the top bar keeps the hole, par and yards and the player who is up (with a count of the others), the shot controls are the shot line, the club, the curve, height and spin (three buttons in a row; tap spin to step it round), the lie and the Swing button, **three number tiles run across the top** beside the wind with a launch monitor armed — carry, total and backspin unless you pick others in Shot data; on the Swing Meter the meter shows carry, total and apex instead — and the cameras fold behind one camera button under the wind. Tools is in the top bar. Aim by tapping the course to get close, then finish with the **aim pad** in the bottom-right corner, under the map: **‹ ›** turn the line half a degree a tap, **˄ ˅** move the target a yard further or shorter — on the green they make the putt longer or shorter — and the flag in the middle aims straight at the pin. Hold any arrow to sweep. **Tap the map** and it opens big over the course: tap where you want to aim, pinch with two fingers to zoom in for precision, drag to look around, and press **Done**. Swing with the big **Swing** button: it starts the meter, which runs under the number tiles, and the next two taps can land anywhere on the screen. Use **Skip animation** to jump to where it lands. Held sideways, the meter sits at the foot of the screen between the shot controls and the map, and the club's carry is read off the bar's full line rather than printed under the club. Buttons are sized for a thumb, a double tap never zooms the page, and pulling down never reloads it and loses your round. The tee you play from is chosen in the round's **Format & tees** rather than in the panel. Held sideways, everything keeps clear of the notch and the home bar. Panels you have dragged somewhere stay where you put them on every screen.

**On a phone, put it on your home screen.** Open the game's web address in Safari, tap Share, then **Add to Home Screen**; the icon opens Fairway full screen like an app. On Android, use Chrome's **Install app**. The game needs a web address for this: open it from the link run_fairway_server prints for a phone (the download's README), or from any web host you put it on. The fullscreen button is hidden on an iPhone, where browsers cannot go fullscreen — the home-screen icon is how an iPhone plays it full screen. The first time you open the hosted game in a browser on an iPhone or iPad, the menu tells you how, once.

Starting a **Sim drop** puts the Tools window away, so the drop bar and **Place ball** are never underneath it.

**Escape closes the panel in front of you first.** If a tool window is open behind the scorecard or any other panel, Escape closes the panel and leaves the tool window where it was. A single tap of an arrow key always nudges the aim or the aim point, however quick the tap and however slow the machine. A swing in progress is in front of everything: Escape cancels it first, before the distance is set.

**Panels move and resize at all times**, with no mode to enter first. Drag the shot controls, weather, course map, camera strip or free-flight controls by the title bar across the top, the same bar a tool window has, and pull the bottom corner to resize. On a phone the title bars are left off to save height, and a small grip in the corner does the same job. Focus a handle and use arrow keys for 10-pixel adjustments, or Shift+arrows for 1 pixel. Layout saves separately on this browser, adapts to screen size, and **Reset panel layout** in the tools tray restores the defaults. A layout saved before the play screen was redrawn in September is set aside once, because the shot controls moved from the bottom edge to the left. Panels can scroll when resized small.

Building a course takes a few seconds and the page cannot respond while it happens, so a **"Growing your landscape…"** message covers the wait. Nine holes are quicker than eighteen, and trees, houses and waterways all add to it.

Fairway opens on a **starting screen** — the mark on black while the renderer comes up and the first hole grows — and then on a **main menu**, with the camera circling a single showcase hole — a different one each visit, at a different time of day between dawn and golden hour -- never at night, so set the clock in a round if you want to play under the floodlights; nothing is generated for you and no round starts until you ask. From there: continue the round you were playing, **Play**, the **Course of the week** or **Challenges** (see below), or open **Course studio**. On a phone held upright the cards sit two to a row, with Continue or Play across the top. Play chooses a saved course, your group, the format and your tee, and is also where you **delete** a course you no longer want or reach **Manage & share courses** — the course decides the landscape and how many holes it has, so there is nothing about terrain to set here. **Surprise me & play** skips the choosing and builds a fresh randomised nine on the spot.

**Driving range** is a practice ground rather than a hole: 500 yards deep, 100 yards wide, dead flat, and mown from behind the mats all the way to the back of the field. It is the same range every visit — nothing about it is drawn from a seed — so two shots hit a week apart are comparable. There is no round and no score; the only thing it gives back is what the ball did. A real green sits out on the centre line, and the **Green distance** slider in the tools tray slides it anywhere from 30 to 300 yards so you can practise a particular number — the painted surface, the flag, the cup and every distance readout move with it, and the field stays mown to 500 yards behind wherever it ends up. Six targets run down the field at 50 to 300 yards, alternating sides, each a coloured circle with a white rim, an oversized flag in the same colour and a board behind it carrying the number — the colour tells you which one you are looking at before you read the board, and the boards grow with distance so the far ones stay legible from the mats. The targets are markings rather than putting surfaces: a ball landing on one takes a range-turf bounce, and the movable green is the one that plays as a green.

Hitting is a loop: the ball returns to the mat after every shot, the club you picked stays in your hands, and the tracers stay on the field behind you so a session reads as a session. **Shot lines** in the range controls decides how many of those tracers stay up — anywhere from none, for a clear field, to the last 50 — and it applies as you drag it. A session always keeps the full 50 behind the scenes, so raising the slider brings back lines you have already hit rather than only affecting the next shot. Nothing is scored — the hole card just says Range, par N/A, and the live distance to the green. Everything the shot did goes to **Shot information**: carry, total, offline, apex, ball speed, launch angle, spin and spin axis, with a running average carry and offline spread across the session. Offline is measured across the line you aimed down rather than from the green, so aiming at a target and hitting it counts as straight. Every shot is recorded against the golfer who hit it, and clicking the shot number on a player's card — or **Shot data** in the tools tray — opens that golfer's session list. The lab records into the same list, naming each row by what fired it: the preset, the launch-monitor line, or the putt distance. Every row has a **Replay** button that flies that shot again — the trajectory that actually happened, not a fresh simulation, so moving the green or changing the firmness afterwards cannot change what you are shown. The last 50 shots of a session stay replayable; older rows keep their numbers.

**Lab tools** is a popup in the driving range, not a mode of its own. It carries three things: **green firmness**, which decides what a ball does the moment it lands — how high it bounces and whether it holds; **green speed** in Stimp, which decides what it does once it is rolling; and a box to **fire a shot** from typed numbers rather than a swing. Give it ball speed, launch angle, direction, spin and spin axis — or paste Open Connect JSON straight from a launch monitor — and that shot goes through the same simulator a swing goes through. Direction and spin axis mean what a launch monitor means by them: positive is right (a ball started right, a fade), negative is left. Nothing is invented: the five numbers you give are the five numbers the ball leaves with, and the shot is recorded in your shot data as *Launch monitor*. Both turf settings apply immediately, to the shot in front of you and every one after it.

There used to be a separate **Lab** mode with its own flat green, a floating control bar and sixteen preset shots to click. The range replaced the need for it — it is already flat, mown end to end, and its green moves to any stated number — so the mode is gone and what was actually being reached for is a popup. The measurement half survives in the browser console as a `lab` object: `lab.batch`, `lab.drops`, `lab.scatter`, `lab.lipSweep` and `lab.envelope` run sweeps against the loaded course without animating them, which is how carry, release, firmness and bounce behaviour were fitted in the first place. Time can still be slowed to a twentieth and the camera can still sit on the turf beside the cup.

The main menu stays behind whichever dialogue you open from it — the wordmark and the landscape remain, and the mode tiles step aside for the sheet that replaced them. The round dialogue offers **Start fresh round** only when you are not already playing one; opened mid-round it is there to change your group, reach your saved rounds and export the one in progress, and starting over is a main-menu decision. Every mode opens the same way: a tabbed dialogue that asks what you want before anything is built, with the way in — *Start fresh round*, *Start an endless run*, *Open the driving range*, *Grow this landscape* — at the right-hand end of the tab row, so it is on screen whichever tab you are reading.

On the range the map also carries **dispersion**: one ellipse per club, drawn from the shots you actually hit, with a dot for every finish and the group's centre marked. It covers every ball that club hit, and it lies along the way the club actually misses rather than along the aim line — long and short is a different miss from left and right, and most clubs group about half as wide as they are deep. The legend gives both axes, long first. A club needs three shots before it draws. It is a read of what happened and not a model of what might: the simulator adds no miss of its own, so a club fed identical numbers ten times groups on a point. Toggle it and read the shot counts in Range controls.

**Shot data** in the tools tray decides which numbers the **number tiles** show — on screen with a launch monitor armed (on the Swing Meter the meter shows carry, total and apex instead, and the playing-by-hand set is kept but shown nowhere) — one set for playing by hand, one for an armed launch monitor and one for a phone (three at most: carry, total and backspin to start with), chosen at the top of the panel. Everything you hit
stays there until you hit the next one, so you can look at a drive while you walk up to it rather
than watching the numbers vanish the moment the ball stops. While a ball is in the air the panel is
already showing it: the launch numbers at once, and carry and total counting up with the ball —
a reading of where the ball has got to, never a forecast of where it will finish. **Every number is a tile of its own** — a small box, the number in bold, sized to your screen — and you can drag each one anywhere on the course; it stays where you leave it, separately for playing by hand, with a monitor and on a phone. Tiles you have not moved sit in a row along the foot of the course, between the shot controls and the map — a second row above if they do not fit — and on a phone in a row across the top. The **shot line** at the top of the shot controls (or of the launch monitor window) carries the shot's state, Skip and the countdown, and its chevron hides or shows all the tiles. **Reset layout** in Tools puts every tile back. A number you ticked always shows; one your monitor did not send is a dash.

The panel starts with the eight numbers every shot has: ball speed, launch angle, launch direction,
spin, spin axis, carry, total and apex. Beyond those, anything your launch monitor sends can go on
it — club speed, smash factor, attack angle, club path, face to target, dynamic loft, lie, closure
rate, where on the face you hit it, the backspin and sidespin split, and the monitor's own carry
and total alongside ours. There is also face to path, which no monitor sends because it is face
minus path, worked out for you. Tick up to twelve of them. 

Numbers that need a monitor show a dash without one rather than a zero, and the monitor's distances
are labelled as the monitor's, because they will not agree with ours and you should be able to see
which is which.

**Every course has a name**, and the box is always filled in before you get there. Names are built from
words that belong to the landscape — *Tradewind Cove* on an island, *Marram Braes* on links, *Copper
Hollow* in autumn — and the same course always suggests the same name, so a name you turn down and
come back to is the one you left. *Surprise me & play* takes the suggestion without asking, since the
point of that button is not choosing anything. Anywhere you save or share a course the name comes with
it, and a course cannot be saved without one.

**Copy course code**, in the Tools window under *This course*, puts the whole course on your clipboard as a share code — the same code Saved courses produces, carrying the name and every setting that shapes the ground, so whoever pastes it grows the identical course. An endless run has no code: it grows a new hole each time, so there is nothing to keep. Panels share one set of corners and shadows, and text follows one rule: a serif face for the course title, panel headings and the large numbers you glance at, and a sans face for every label, button and table.

**Every panel on screen moves and resizes, whenever you want.** The shot controls, the weather, the map and the camera strip each carry a title bar like a tool window's, with a resize handle in the bottom corner — drag the bar, pull the corner, or focus either and use the arrow keys. (On a phone, a small grip in the corner stands in for the bar.) Where you put them is remembered, in every mode. There is no arrange mode to enter; *Reset panel layout* in the tools tray puts everything back.

The **hole map** scrolls and zooms in every mode, the studio included: the wheel zooms about the pointer, dragging pans, and a double-click returns it to the fitted view. The title shows the zoom factor whenever the map is not showing the whole hole or the whole course. On a touch screen two fingers pinch it to zoom. Clicking still aims — a drag is not treated as a click — and the map returns to its fitted view whenever a new hole loads. The small button in the map's bottom-right corner opens the map **big**, over the course, to aim on; on a touch screen a tap anywhere on the small map does the same, because on a thumbnail a fingertip covers twenty yards. The big map aims, places a drop or flies wherever the small one would, opens on the whole hole, and closes with **Done** or Escape — or on its own when a shot is struck or a panel opens.

**Shot views** draws the shot flat, in the two shapes the 3D view cannot make: height against distance down the line, and the ground track across it. It is a tool you open rather than something on screen while you hit. Height is measured from where you struck the ball, as if the ground were flat, so the curve is the flight itself rather than its clearance over each bump, and its peak is the same apex the shot reports; a ball that finishes on a green above the tee ends above the line, one that runs down a slope ends below it. The lower plot is the shot seen from above with the ball travelling left to right, so a ball that went right of your aim bends down the plot and one that went left bends up. Its dashed centre line is the line you aimed down, not the line to the green, so a shot aimed at a target and hit straight reads straight. It follows your last shot on its own; pressing **View** on a row in your shot data pins it to that shot until you hit the next one.

**Range controls** opens by itself when you arrive at the range, with the green distance and shot-line sliders in it. Close it and it comes back from the **Tools** window, which carries a **Range controls** button whenever you are on the range. **Swing Meter** (in the shot controls and the tools tray) sets the difficulty of whoever is up and says what the shot's shape, spin and lie do. **Turf settings** holds the ground it all lands on — green speed, the roll settings and firmness.

**Growing a course no longer freezes the page.** The generator hands the browser a frame as it works, so the waiting card turns, names what it is doing — routing the holes, shaping the land, cutting the water, building the ground, planting — and fills a bar that means something: the ground alone is four fifths of the job, and the reading is weighted so it advances evenly rather than crawling and then leaping. It costs about a quarter more time overall than locking the page solid did.

**A hole can be given something to play around.** *Feature tree or rocks in a fairway* sets how often a hole gets a specimen standing in its own short grass — the lone tree, the outcrop of stones — the way a famous hole often does. It is sited to be fair rather than cheap: there is always open grass down at least one side, it keeps well back from the green, it only appears where the hole is wide enough to take one, and it never stands close enough to a tee to leave you no shot. A specimen is a tree, a cactus in the desert, or a cluster of stones — never a shrub: on a links, where gorse and heather are the only things that grow, it is always stones. Set it to zero for clean fairways.

**Boulders stop a ball.** Rocks are part of the course now rather than scenery painted on it, so a stone in the rough is something to play around instead of something to roll through — they run up to six metres across on mountain and desert. Haunted Hollow's giant pumpkins and toadstools stop a ball the same way; its ghosts do not, and a ball flies straight through one. Floodlight masts stop a ball too, but only when the lights are actually on and the masts are standing there to be seen. Fallen logs and stumps in the redwoods are still scenery.

**Green shape** and **Bunker shape** set how far a green or a bunker departs from an oval. Everything used to be a smooth oval; turned up, greens grow lobes and pinched waists and sand goes ragged. Raising a slider also evens out the waves that build the outline, so no single one takes over — letting one dominate is what turns a green into a tidy three-lobed flower instead of something a groundsman would recognise. Put either back to zero and you get the rounded outlines the game always had. One thing to know: shaping a green makes the ground around it steeper, noticeably so on hilly settings.

**Green definition** and **Green mowing bands**, in Graphics under *Reading the ground*, decide how easily you can read the shape of a green without turning on the green-reading overlays. A green is the flattest ground on the course, so it has the least shape to show. Definition lets the shading and the grass sheen treat the green as steeper than it is and bends its mowing bands to follow the surface, the way a contour line does — it changes nothing about the ground itself, so the ball rolls exactly where it always did. Green mowing bands sets how strong the light and dark cut bands are. At the default *Green slope & difficulty* a green is genuinely gentle — about half a percent of shape on top of its overall tilt — so it will read as gentle; raising that setting in the course studio gives greens more shape, and changes how they putt.

**Trees close to greens** sets how far planting comes in around a green. The side your shot comes in from stays clear at every setting — there is always somewhere to land — and the slider moves the trees behind and to the sides. At zero they stand well back; at full they close in on the collar, so a green can sit in a clearing or in a pocket of woodland.

**Tees sit in the trees, not in a clearing.** That holds however wide you set the fairways — the planting round a tee is measured against the hole it belongs to rather than a fixed distance, so a wide course does not end up with its tees in clearings. Planting comes right up to the mown apron behind and beside a tee box, so it is framed by whatever grows on that course instead of standing in a bare circle. Only the ground in front is kept open — and it is judged from the whole tee complex at once, so a tree cannot sit in the view from the back tee just because it misses that tee’s own line — and tree lines everywhere thin out towards the short grass rather than stopping along a hard line.

**You always have a shot from the tee.** Nothing solid is allowed to stand where your tee shot would fly through it — not a trunk, a boulder or a fallen log. It is judged on the line the hole actually asks for, so a par three is measured to the green rather than to some notional drive, and on height as well as distance: a tall tree is refused far down the hole, while a rock the ball sails thirty metres over is left where it is. Only the ground in FRONT of a tee is kept clear — trees beside and behind the box are deliberately left, because they are what makes a tee look like somewhere rather than a platform.

**A full shot is filmed the way television films one.** The camera you aimed from stays where it is and turns to follow the ball, so you watch it climb and curve across the sky with its tracer behind it — the shape of the shot. Only once the ball is well past its highest point does it cut to a second camera already standing beside where the ball will land, which watches it come down, bounce and roll out. A chip or a pitch that lands within about seventy yards stays on one camera, which eases in behind the ball and follows it; on a longer shot the landing camera, once the ball is down, swings round behind it to watch it run out to where it stops; a putt keeps its own camera beside the line. When the ball stops, **the camera flies up to show the whole shot** from the side, tee to rest, holds it while the panel counts down, then flies on to your next shot -- straight through any trees in the way, both going out and coming back. Enter skips it; **Show the whole shot when the ball stops** in Camera settings turns it off, and **Follow the ball in flight** turns both cameras off and leaves the view where you aimed from. **G** flies the camera to your aim point, then to the green, then back.

**A new hole arrives.** Reaching a hole you have not played — teeing off a round, walking to the next hole, or the next hole of an endless run — opens above and behind the tee, looking down the length of it. It holds there for a couple of seconds so you can read the shape and where the trouble is, then flies down onto your ball — slowly, over four or five seconds, and straight through any trees in the way rather than climbing over them (a tree the camera passes through disappears while it is inside it). Resuming a hole you are already halfway down skips it, and so does the driving range.

**The camera flies between views rather than cutting.** Moving to the overview or the green view and back, stepping out of a flyover, and every way into a round — an endless run, a new course, continuing a saved round, the driving range, or coming down out of the studio — are all flown: the camera lifts, crosses, and settles on the shot, easing out of the move and into it so it starts and stops at rest. It keeps above the ground and above the treetops on the way, and short hops behind the ball are left alone rather than turned into a journey.

**The camera** stays with you for a moment and a half after the strike before it goes after the ball — a putt it follows immediately, because the roll is the whole event. It trails from off your shoulder rather than from straight behind, and along the line to the hole rather than the line you struck on, so the flag stays in shot on anything that curves. The closer the ball works toward the hole the tighter it frames, all the way down to turf level for a ball trickling up to the cup.

**Morning fog & haze** and **Floodlight the course** sit in the clock panel beside the time controls — the first of the weather settings. Floodlights turn on night golf. Poles run down alternating sides of every hole, with a pair flanking the back of every green at 45° either side of the line you play in on, placed the way a sports field is lit — 23 m masts, spaced within three times their own height, set 18 yd beyond the semi rough along the fairway and 10 yd past it around the greens, so they are never in play. Every round starts with them off -- nothing is built into the skyline and nothing is lit until you ask for it -- and switching them on is instant, because everything they need is prepared on the loading screen. A round started from Play (a saved course, one fresh from Course studio, or Surprise me) begins at midday, unless **Start at my local time** is ticked, and so does opening Course studio; Endless keeps the hour of the hole it grows from. Below them, **Floodlight strength** and **Glow ball strength** set how bright the lamps and the glow ball are, from 0 to 200% of how Fairway tunes them (100%); both apply at once and are kept with your other time settings. The clock panel scrolls if your screen is too short to show all of it.

**Endless** plays the hole you are already looking at. Choosing it asks for your format, tees, putting mode and group first — there is no course to pick, since every hole is grown from the run's own seed, and match play is not offered because a match ends when the lead exceeds the holes remaining and an endless run has no last hole. The showcase hole on the menu is hole one of a real endless run, so starting one simply tees it up — no wait, no regeneration — and each time you hole out the next hole grows from the same seed. A run is one seed and a hole number, so it can be put down and picked up later without storing a course.

**Course studio** is its own mode. Fly over the landscape with W/A/S/D (no aim line is drawn here: nobody is about to hit), open **Settings** to change anything about how it grows, then press **Regenerate** to rebuild it. Settings are grouped as Course, Landscape, Routing, Fairways &amp; greens, Bunkers, Ponds &amp; lakes, Rivers &amp; creeks, Trees &amp; scenery and Weather, and every one has an **ⓘ** explaining what it changes — or press **Show all descriptions** to read the lot. Nothing rebuilds on its own — a course takes seconds to grow — so the bar tells you when settings have changed and the landscape is out of date. While a panel is open the bar sits just above it, so Regenerate stays in reach beside the sliders; on a phone held sideways it shrinks to its icons. **Save course** names it and puts it in your library. **Menu** in the top bar returns here at any time.

**Course studio → Saved courses** keeps a library of landscapes on this device. Name a course you like and it is there next time; **Play** puts you straight back on it, and **Rename** changes what it is called without touching the landscape. The save button names what it would save — *Save "Midwest"* while you are playing it, *Save this landscape* in the studio — and when there is nothing to save, at the main menu or during an endless run, it says so and offers Course studio instead. A saved course holds its landscape only, so the same course works for any group, format or tee. **Get code** turns one into a short `FW1.…` code you can paste to someone else, and **Import code** adds theirs to your library — it carries no scores, no round in progress, and none of your own club or turf settings. A code from a newer Fairway is refused rather than half-read, and a course built by a different generator is flagged in the list.

**Continue and Saved rounds are two different things.** *Continue* on the main menu picks up the one round you were last playing — it keeps itself, with no naming and no list, and there is only ever one. *Saved rounds*, in the round dialogue's own tab, is the shelf you put rounds on deliberately: leaving a round you have started offers to save it there under a name, and each one resumes to the same lie and the same scores. Choosing **leave and let it go** really does let it go — the round is not waiting for you the next time you open Fairway. The showcase hole behind the main menu is never saved as a round.

**A round remembers how you were playing it, not just where the ball was.** Turf conditions travel with a saved, exported and resumed round, endless runs included. Your bag travels with you instead: it is in your profile, and in the players' backup file.

**Each saved round exports on its own**, from its row in the list, under a filename taken from its name — and each can be renamed there too. The separate *Export the round you are playing* is for the one in your hands, and is offered only while there is one.

**Importing a round is entering it.** If you are in the middle of a round, Fairway asks whether to save that one first, exactly as leaving by any other route does; once the file is read you are put straight into the imported round rather than left on the menu.

Saved and exported rounds record which course generator built them. When a Fairway update changes how courses grow, reopening an older round asks whether to continue on the rebuilt landscape or start a fresh round on the same course, rather than quietly moving the ground under your ball. Scores are kept either way. Settings themselves are upgraded automatically, so an older save never has to be rebuilt by hand.

Water and out-of-bounds still incur one penalty stroke and replay from the previous lie. This simplified stroke-and-distance rule applies to water; lateral-drop and full tournament rules are not implemented.

**Camera & bay** is on the main menu as well as in the tools tray, and everything in it stays on this device — it survives a reload, and it is never carried inside a course or a round you share.

**Simulator bay** turns the field of view from a look into a measurement. Tell it your screen's **width and height** in inches, measured on the wall, and how far back you stand, and it works out the one vertical angle that makes the course line up with the room: the screen is a window, and there is exactly one angle that makes what is on it match what would be there if the wall were not. With it on, the camera stands where you stand — directly behind the ball with no sideways offset at all, at your eye height, with the ball a real distance in front of you. The ball can sit below the bottom of the frame; it is below your eyeline in the room too. If your mat is not in front of the middle of the screen, set **Your mat, left or right of the screen centre** (in feet, negative for left): the camera keeps looking straight down your target line, and the picture slides sideways the way a projector's lens shift does, so the line lands on the screen exactly where it is in the room. The panel shows the angle your bay gives and the screen's shape, worked out from the two sides (16:9, 4:3, or a ratio when it is neither), and for a hand-set angle it tells you how far back you would have to stand to justify it. The picture is drawn in your screen's shape, not the game window's, because the projector stretches the window onto the screen; resizing the window does not change it, and the panel warns you when the window on your own display is a different shape (so it looks stretched there, and lines up full screen on the projector). The mat setting moves in tenths of a foot. A bay saved as a diagonal before 5 October is converted to its two sides automatically.

**Reading the ground** is five switches in Graphics and the two green sliders above, because a cast shadow only tells you anything when the sun is low. **Ground shading** lifts slopes facing the sun and darkens slopes facing away, as if the sun were always low, plus a lift on crowns and a shade in hollows worked out when the course is built — it comes from the same side as the real shadows, and it is what makes a fairway read as a surface rather than a sheet at noon. **Slope tinting** dries turf on slopes and keeps hollows lush, the way water actually runs off; it works in colour rather than brightness, and turf that is already dry — desert scrub, links fescue — has nothing left to lose and barely changes. **Grass sheen** makes mown turf lighter where it tips away from you and darker where it tips toward you, which is how real turf shows its slopes at any hour; strongest on fairways, and only a gentle hint on greens. **Contour lines** draw a topographic line every metre of height across the whole course — frankly artificial, and the easiest thing here to read, because it turns a slope into a spacing you can count. **Mowing stripes** are the cut bands that bend over a roll, and the clean-up lap the mower makes round the edge of every green. Everything here is on by default except contours, and none of it costs a measurable frame.

**The look of the course** is four sliders in Graphics, on every tier, and none costs a measurable frame. **Turf colour variation** (60% by default) runs every surface in patches the way a real course has them -- broad drifts in the rough, worn and watered patches on a fairway, damp sand in a bunker, and only the faintest change of tone on a green. **Shade under trees and rocks** (60%) is a soft darkening at the foot of every trunk and boulder, deeper under a forest where the crowns overlap. **Distance haze** (50%) fades far land toward the sky's colour, warmer toward the sun, and much less when you look down from above. **Wind in the trees and grass** (100%) is how far trees, leaves and grass move; gusts sweep across the course as fronts you can watch travel downwind -- a crosswind gust bends the trees on one side of a fairway before the other -- and each tree bends as a whole, more at the top, swaying slower the taller it is (a cactus not at all). It changes the picture only, never the ball.

**Costs a frame** is the group below it, and they are labelled that way because they are real work on every frame: **terrain casts shadows** (ridges shading the hollows behind them, which only says anything when the sun is low) and **terrain casts shadows** alone. **Cloud shadows** (high and ultra) belong to the clouds you can actually see overhead rather than to a noise field: each cloud's disc is projected along the sun, so the patch of shade sits offset from the cloud the way a real one does, follows the terrain for free and drifts with the same wind. It adds no render pass — it darkens direct sunlight inside the materials that are already being drawn, and leaves ambient alone, because a cloud blocks the sun and not the sky. **Water reflections** chooses whether ponds, lakes and creeks reflect the course around them or only the sky. Neither costs anything per frame; with it on, each body's reflection is photographed when the course is built and again whenever the sun moves about six degrees, a pause of around 40–65 ms each time, and switching it off skips that entirely. **Floodlight shadows** (on by default) let the lamps nearest your shot throw shadows when the course is floodlit -- five lamps, or three on High and Ultra -- redrawn only when the lamps or the view move, so they cost nothing frame to frame; the first time you change that switch on a course it takes a few seconds to prepare while you play on, and the game says when it lands. The surface is the same either way: clear where you look into it, sky where you look across it, and ripples generated from the world position rather than sampled from a texture, so there is no tile to spot at pond scale.

**On the green the camera comes down behind your ball.** Aiming a putt, it stands about six and a half metres back and a little over two metres up, looking along your line at the green just past the cup, so the ball sits low in the picture and the green fills it (with **Simulator bay** on, the view is your room's, as before). While the ball rolls, the camera looks between the ball and the cup -- and at the ball itself once it runs past or wide -- so the ball never leaves the picture.

**A rolling putt keeps both the ball and the hole in view.** It is the same camera as every other shot — same height, same distance, same angle — looking between the ball and the cup while the ball rolls, and at the ball itself if it runs past or wide, so it never leaves the picture.

**Which view you are looking through is on the tools tray, not in the settings.** Behind the ball, whole-course overview, a look at the green and free flight are four buttons that are on screen the whole time, and each toggles back to the ball view. The camera panel is only for how the view is set up — it has no view picker of its own, because the view you are currently looking through is not a setting.

Camera settings adjust height above the ball, distance behind it, horizontal offset and field of view — or, with **Simulator bay** on, they take your bay's measurements instead. There is no separate putting camera: the view you set up is the view you keep, on the green as much as anywhere. Horizontal offset translates both the camera and its target equally, preserving the viewing direction. Press **V** or the bird icon for free flight: **W/A/S/D** moves, **R/F** rises/descends, **Shift** boosts speed, and dragging or arrow keys turns the camera. Scroll to fly forward/backward. Jump to any green using the hole selector, click the whole-course map, or use the on-screen movement buttons. **Return to ball**, **V**, or **C** restores the appropriate ball view, including the close putting camera on a green, without changing the current shot. Distance to pin stays in the bottom controls (and free-flight bar), using feet close to the cup and yards farther away. Camera settings also include free-flight speed. The round automatically saves after completed shots. Export/import a saved round to transfer it to another browser, and download the scorecard as CSV. File-URL storage behavior varies by browser; use export for a reliable portable save.

The moment a golfer's score for a hole is final, **its name lands in the middle of the screen** -- *Hole in one*, *Eagle*, *Birdie*, *Par*, *Bogey* up to *Quadruple bogey*, and past that the number over par, such as **+5**. Under it are the strokes, the par and the score against par, tinted green under and red over like the score chips. It comes about a second after the ball drops (a ball that finishes anywhere else holds the camera on it for three seconds; one in the cup has nothing left to look at), rises in, holds and lifts away in under three seconds, before the scorecard opens. It works the same in every format: in stroke and match play it names the golfer (when there is more than one), in a scramble the team, once the team's score is settled; in an endless run it shows on every hole. A decimal-putting score with a fraction has no name, so it shows the number (**+0.5**). The range never shows it -- there is no score there.

When a hole is holed out, the camera pulls back into a slow orbit of the whole hole with **every tracer of that hole drawn at once**, so you can see the way it was played while the scorecard is up. Each line is drawn in its golfer's own tracer -- its colours and its shape, held still: a dashed tracer stays dashed, a lightning one zigzags. During play a tracer clears once its shot is over rather than hanging over the next one.

The **score chips** in the top bar are a live score for everyone in the group: how each stands against par, tinted behind the number — progressively red over par, green under — and the shot they are on, or *In* once they have holed out. The one who is up is lit. The scorecard shows the same in full. Beside the chips the top bar shows par, the yardage, which pin day the cups are cut for and whether this hole is front, middle or back. On a practice ground a chip counts shots instead, and tapping another golfer's chip hands them the mat; tapping the one who is up opens their shot list.

**A scramble chooses its ball on the course, not from a list.** Once every teammate has hit, all of the team's balls stay on the course where they lie, each in the colour of the golfer who hit it and each under a beam so it can be found from the far side of a fairway. The bar at the foot of the screen steps between them, putting the camera behind each ball looking down its line to the pin — the view the next shot would actually be played from — and the check mark takes the one you are looking at. It opens on the ball nearest the pin, which is usually but not always the one you want. A list could tell you "Alex, 148 yd"; it could not tell you that Alex is behind a bunker.

**Any golfer can be removed from a round in progress**, including the one whose turn it is and including the first name in the list. The Apply button names who is leaving and asks for a second press — their scorecard goes with them and there is no undo. Whoever is left keeps their own card, their own strokes and their own ball; the turn passes on if the golfer who was up has gone.

**In stroke play each golfer plays the hole out before the next one starts**, and the next tee is played in the order they scored the last one — **lowest score has honours**. Golfers who tied keep the order they already had, and anyone who joined mid-round and has no score for that hole goes to the back. Best-ball match play still plays the ball farthest from the pin shot by shot, with honours breaking the tie on the tee where every ball is the same distance away. Scramble teams alternate from their shared lie, as they always have.

**Every golfer in the group has a colour** — gold, cyan, indigo, magenta, by their position in the group. It is the dot on the edge of their circle in the top bar, the dot on their scorecard row, the dot on their row in the group editor (beside their ball; once a launch monitor has connected, each golfer's row takes two lines, the name across the top and how they play beneath), so the name on the card and the chip in the top bar are recognisably the same person. (Tracers are each golfer's own choice, in every mode -- see *Your look*.) Red and green are never used for a golfer: those belong to over and under par on the same cards.

At least three seconds after everyone finishes a hole -- and never while the XP popup or a level-up banner is still up: it waits for them to go -- the scorecard shows each score and putt total, including fractional automatic putts. It advances after eight seconds; **Stay on scorecard** pauses it, and **Next hole now** skips the wait. Final rounds stay on the final card. Scorecard CSVs include putts. Putting statistics for shots from older saves were not recorded and cannot be reconstructed.

**Ball & turf** also sets green Stimp (6–15 ft) and fairway/semi-rough/rough roll (30–180%). Higher percentages produce more run. Defaults are Stimp 10 and 100% roll. Putter bag distance is calibrated at Stimp 10; faster/slower greens change its real distance. Turf conditions affect measured monitor shots as well as manual shots.

## The Swing Meter

Playing with the keyboard, the mouse, a touch screen or a controller, you swing with the **Swing Meter**: a bar along the foot of the course, three taps, the way Hot Shots Golf and PGA 2K do it.

1. **Tap to start.** The marker sets off from the far left of the bar, runs up to the strike line and on up the bar. A tap before it reaches the strike line does nothing.
2. **Tap to set the distance.** The bar is marked in **yards of carry** for the club in your hands -- its full line is your bag's yardage for that club -- so to carry 60 of your 100-yard wedge, stop the marker at 60. **For a short shot the bar zooms**: when your aim circle is well inside the club's carry, the full line becomes a **half swing** or a **quarter swing** (the meter says which), so a chip fills most of the bar instead of a sliver of it, and a zoomed bar runs a little slower. Past the full line is a short orange **overswing** zone worth up to 5% more carry, at the price of a much smaller strike zone. **Once the marker passes the full line it slows right down and the meter flashes**, and at the end of the zone it stops and waits, still flashing, until you tap -- so how deep you go is your choice.
3. **Tap on the strike line.** The marker comes back; tap it inside the yellow **strike zone** and the ball goes where you aimed. **Early pushes the ball right, late pulls it left** -- for everyone, whichever way you swing -- and the further off the line, the bigger the miss. The narrow green band in the middle is a **perfect strike**. Let the marker run past the zone and it is a bad miss. **The bar keeps your swing while the ball is moving**: where you set the distance (the dashed gold line) and where you struck stay on it through the flight, and the bar is clean again once the ball stops.

**Space** taps it, so does **A / Cross** on a controller, and a click or touch on **Swing** or on the bar starts it; once a swing has started, **a tap anywhere on the screen** counts. **Escape** cancels a swing before the distance is set, with no stroke. A tap counts the moment it happens, however fast or slow the screen is drawing.

**The meter carries its own numbers.** On the Swing Meter the floating number tiles are gone; once you make the final tap the meter stays up while the ball flies, with **carry, total and apex** counting up under the bar. The row never goes away: it keeps your last shot until your next swing starts, shows dashes while you swing, and shows dashes for the next golfer up or on a new hole, so nothing jumps while you line up. With a launch monitor armed the tiles are back, as before.

**The blue marker on the bar is where to stop** for the ball to come down in your aim circle. The game works it out by flying the ball: it allows for the slope up or down to the circle, the lie, and the air (altitude and temperature) -- and **on Easy, the wind too**. On Normal and Hard the wind is yours to judge from the wind dial. If even a full swing falls short, the marker sits on the full line in red and the meter says *Out of reach: take more club*; it never points into the overswing zone. **Changing club moves the circle** to that club's carry down the fairway, unless you have placed it yourself this turn (clicked, moved it with ↑ ↓ or the pad, or typed a distance), in which case the circle stays and the marker moves. Turned the line with ← → but left the distance alone? The new club's carry is taken along your line.

**A putt is two taps** on every difficulty: start, then stop at the length you want. The bar is in feet or yards of roll on a flat green at today's green speed, and it zooms to the putt -- a quarter or a half of your putter's roll for a short one, running a little slower, so you get a bar you can actually stop on, and on past the putter's full roll for a long one, far enough to putt across any green on the slowest green speed. **There is no putt preview**: the line runs straight from the ball to where you aim, for every way of playing, and the break is yours to read from the green -- the grid, the flow and the heat map on the course map. The aim circle sets the length you are trying to hit, and the blue marker is that length on a flat green; the slope is yours to allow for.

**Shape the shot before you swing.** Beside the club are three choices, which reset every shot:

- **Curve** (**S**): Draw, Straight or Fade. The aim line bends to show it, and the shot is started the other way by as much as it curves, so a good strike still comes down on your line.
- **Height** (**W**): Low, Normal or High, chosen separately from the curve. Near the green with a wedge, Low becomes **Bump & run** and High becomes **Flop**.
- **Spin** (**X**, **Shift+X** for less): the club's own spin, a step less, or up to three steps more. **Every step of spin you add narrows the strike zone.** With no spin added, the ball never spins back -- it checks and releases. Add spin and what happens on the green is up to you and the turf.

**The lie costs ball speed**, and the shot controls say so before you swing -- *Rough · −10% ball speed*, in red. Semi-rough keeps 96%, rough 90% and sand 80%; the tee, fairway, fringe and green keep it all. On the bar the part the lie takes away is hatched, and the strike zone is narrower in rough and sand. After the shot the ball speed tile shows what the lie took off, in red beside it. Launch-monitor shots lose the same, unless the round switches that off (*Launch monitor setup*).

**Difficulty is your own.** Open **Swing Meter** in the shot controls (or the tools tray) to set it for whoever is up; it is kept in that golfer's profile, so a group can play on different settings.

| | Marker | Strike zone | Wind |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Easy** | slow | wide | the blue marker allows for the wind |
| **Normal** | steady | medium | you judge the wind |
| **Hard** | fast | narrow | you judge the wind |

The Swing Meter replaced the power slider, the Shot shape sliders and the ball-flight profile in October 2026. Your bag decides how far each club goes; nothing else does.

## Water and out of bounds

**A ball in water stops and asks where you want to play from.** Water is a
penalty area, and because nothing on a Fairway course is marked otherwise, every
pond, lake, river and creek is a *red* one. The relief bar at the foot of the
screen offers what the Rules of Golf allow, each for one penalty stroke:

- **Stroke and distance** -- play again from where you hit.
- **Back on the line** -- drop on the far side of the water, keeping the point
  where the ball last crossed into it between you and the hole. Use **+ 10 yd**
  and **- 10 yd** to go further back for a yardage you like.
- **Lateral relief** -- drop within two club-lengths of where it crossed, no
  nearer the hole.

Each option shows how far it leaves you and the lie, and the camera moves behind
that ball. Pick with a click or tap, or the arrow keys, then **Drop here** (or
Enter). Nothing else can be played until you choose; the choice is kept if you
leave and come back.

**Out of bounds** is one penalty stroke and play again from where you hit -- the
Rules of Golf -- and the game does that for you. In **Format & tees**,
**Out of bounds** can instead be set to **Local Rule E-5**, the casual-play rule
many clubs use: you may also drop near where the ball went out, toward this
hole's fairway and no nearer the hole, for **two** penalty strokes. E-5 is not
always possible: from a tee, the whole fairway is nearer the hole than the spot
where the ball went out, so it is stroke and distance again. The setting is
remembered for every round after.

It works the same in stroke play, match play (the farthest ball plays next once
the drop is taken), scramble (the dropped ball is one of the team's choices and
carries its penalty) and endless runs, under every putting mode. A **Mulligan**
takes back the shot and the drop together. The **Sim drop** tool is separate: it
moves a ball anywhere with no penalty, and it waits until relief is taken.

## XP and levels

**Every golfer earns XP**, on a launch monitor or the Swing Meter, from how they play: 10 for finishing a hole; for the score on it, 10 for a par, 30 a birdie, 80 an eagle, 250 an albatross and 300 a hole in one; 5 each for a fairway and a green in regulation; 5 for a shot that comes down **on the number** -- within 5% of the aim distance of where you aimed -- once a hole and never for a putt; 25 for a chip-in and 25 for a putt holed from over 30 feet; 40 for a new personal best; 50 for finishing nine holes or 100 for eighteen; 50 for winning a match or a scramble; and 100 for the first round you finish each day. On the Swing Meter, Easy earns three quarters and Hard half as much again; a monitor golfer earns the amounts as listed. A mulligan takes back what the shot it takes back earned. On the driving range only *on the number* counts, 2 XP a time, up to 50 a day.

**Levels** come from XP: 400 for level 2, 50 more for each level after that, and from level 50 on every level costs 2,850 -- they never stop. Your level is on your circle in the top bar -- **your ball in your frame, the level written over it** -- and beside your name in the group editor, and on **My profile** with how far you are to the next. **Whose turn it is flashes up in the middle of the screen** as the turn starts, with what the top bar says: their circle, name, score against par and which shot. In a group it comes up every time the turn passes to someone else, after any hole-out banner has gone; playing alone, at the start of each hole. Not on the range. When you go up a level a banner says so, after the hole's own banner, with your ball, your title and what the new level brought (*New tracer: Sky*).

**Every time you earn XP a small popup comes up at the top of the screen**: the total, each thing that earned it (*Birdie +30*, *On the number +5*), the Easy or Hard multiplier if one applied, and your level bar filling from where it was to where it is now, running over into the next level when you cross one. Gains made together -- the putt that holed and the hole it finished -- are one popup, and it waits for the hole's score sting to finish, coming up beside the banner as it fades, so the two never sound together; the scorecard waits for it in turn. On a level-up the bar stops at the top, the level-up sound plays by itself, and then it fills on into the new level; the big level-up banner follows once the popup has gone. It sounds as it goes: a quick three-note pluck as it comes up, a low glide as the bar fills, and a rising two-note swell when you cross into a new level.

Golfers who were playing before XP arrived start at level 1 with nothing back-filled from their history; a note says so once.

**Levels unlock looks.** Up to level 50, most levels bring something: a ball finish, a tracer style, a title, a badge, a meter skin or a flourish. Choose what you wear on **My profile**, under **Your look**, on its tabs: **Ball** (eight solid colours from the start -- red, orange, yellow, green, blue, purple, pink, black -- and sixteen finishes from Classic white and Red stripe to Galaxy, Chrome, Gold and Rainbow), **Tracer** (eight plain colours from the start and fifteen styles: Classic, Sky, Comet with a thick head, Sunset, Dashed, Sparkle trail, Ribbon, Ice, Fire, Lightning, Aurora, Starfall, Ghost, Comet tail plus sparkle and Rainbow, each shown as a little flight that moves the way it does), **Title** (Weekend Golfer at level 4 up to Fairway Master at 50, or none; achievements add more), **Frame**, **Meter** and **Flourishes** (what each one is, when it plays and the level that brings it; tap one to watch it).

**Badges** come at levels 1, 5, 10, 15 and so on to 50: Rookie, Club Member (bronze), Club Regular (silver), Single Digit (gold), Scratch (green), Tour Card (platinum), Contender (sapphire), Major Winner (ruby), Hall of Fame (emerald), Legend (obsidian) and Fairway Master (a turning rainbow), which from level 51 reads *Master 51*, *Master 52* and on. Your level number beside your name is filled with your highest badge's colours, and My profile names it under LEVEL. **Frames** go round your picture: nine plain colours from the start, one in each badge's metal, and moving ones further up -- silver and gold sparkle, water and deep water, spring and autumn leaves, flames in four colours, ice, aurora and galaxy -- whose effects break out of the ring: flames rise above your ball and glow round it, glints orbit, ripples spread, leaves drift past. Until you choose one you wear your newest badge's. **Meter skins** (Classic, Wood, Neon, Retro pixel, Chalkboard, Blueprint, Carbon, Gold leaf, Aurora, Champion) change the Swing Meter's look on your turns: the track, its edge, the marker and the colour of a perfect strike. The strike zone stays yellow, the perfect band green and the target marker blue in every skin, and the zones and timing never change. **Flourishes** play over the cup when you hole out, once earned, the rarest that fits, and they start simple: sparkles on a par at level 2, confetti on a birdie at 5, fireworks on an eagle at 12, a golden ring on a chip-in for par or better at 20, fireworks on every birdie at 30, and at 50 **everything in gold** -- every flourish you have, in gold, rather than one more that would outrank the rest. A hole in one has its own, a sky lantern, open from the start but kept secret: it joins the Flourishes tab the first time you make one. Bogeys get none. They make no sound, and they are off when your system asks for reduced motion. Everything you have not earned yet shows in its own colours with a padlock and the level that brings it. Your ball wears its finish on the course, your own shots fly in your tracer, and your title shows under your name on My profile and the scorecard. **Your ball is your profile picture**: on My profile, beside each name on its player tabs, in the group editor and in the level-up banner. When you hole out, the banner shows your ball close up, turning slowly beside the score. If a mulligan takes back the XP that earned something, you go back to the starting look until you earn it again; your choice is remembered.

## Course of the week, achievements and challenges

**The course of the week** is a tile on the main menu: eighteen holes at the default settings, the same course on every copy of Fairway, with no account and no server -- it is grown from the year and the week number. A new one comes every Monday at your midnight, in the next of the nine landscapes, so in nine weeks you visit them all. The tile says which week and landscape it is and your best this week; the panel it opens says which version of the course generator it is for (a later Fairway that grows courses differently grows a different course from the same week) and your best on any week's course. It is **stroke play only** -- the menu tile and the panel both say so -- since the week's score is your strokes for the eighteen. Choose your group and tees and go. The first round you finish each week earns **150 XP**, and beating your own best that week **50** more. Your score for the week is your strokes for the eighteen.

**Achievements** are on My profile, each earned once, open from level 1, each paying XP and some bringing a title you can wear (under *Your look*, *Title*, *From achievements*):

- *First birdie* (a birdie, 50 XP); *Ace* (a hole in one, 200, title *Ace*)
- *Sandy* (25 up-and-downs from a bunker, 150, title *Sandy*) -- the hole finished within one stroke of your last shot from sand; counted as **Sand saves** on My profile
- *Fairway Finder* (10 fairways in one round, 100, title); *Par-3 Sweep* (a birdie on every par 3 in a round, 200)
- *Beat the Index* (a round under your sim handicap -- half of it for nine holes -- 150)
- *Night Owl* (finish a round with the floodlights on, 100, title); *Fog Walker* and *Ghost Hunter* (finish a round in Giant Redwood or Haunted Hollow, 75 each, titles)
- *Globetrotter* (finish a round in all nine landscapes, 250, title); *Centurion* (finish 100 rounds, 300, title)
- *Streak* (finish the course of the week four weeks running, 150); *On the Dot* (50 shots on the number on a course, 100, title) -- counted as **On the number** on My profile

**Challenges** are a tile on the main menu, all four open from the start. Each plays on a real course -- the course of the week or one of your saved courses -- and your group takes turns:

- **Closest to the pin**: five balls each at the course's first par 3. Every ball inside 20 feet earns 10 XP; your nearest is your record, and a new best earns 50.
- **Long drive**: six drives each down the course's longest par 5 (or par 4). Only a drive that stops on the fairway counts; your longest is your record, and a new best earns 50.
- **Up and down**: five drops round a green, the same five on that course every time. Play each out to the hole; water or out of bounds costs two strokes and you play again from the drop, and after eight strokes a drop is picked up. Every drop holed in two or fewer earns 20 XP; your record is the most of five, fewest strokes breaking a tie.
- **Skins game**: nine holes of stroke play for two to four golfers, on a saved nine or a fresh nine built on the spot. The lowest score alone wins the hole's skin; a tie carries it to the next. The scorecard keeps the count. 30 XP a skin.

A bar at the top of the course, beside the shot controls, says which ball you are on and the best so far; when everyone has played, a card shows the results, with *Play it again* and *Main menu*. The first three are practice games, like the range: nothing goes on a scorecard, your round history or your handicap, there are no mulligans, and they earn only their own XP. A skins game is an ordinary round as well. Your records are on My profile under *Challenge records*.

The ones that count up show how far along you are. An achievement shows in the XP popup when it is earned; one that brings a title says so. Rounds count when they are finished -- an endless run never is. Nothing before achievements arrived is counted back.

## Your profile

**The first time Fairway opens in a browser, it asks your name.** That makes
you the **main profile**: player 1 in every new group, and the player the menu
and **My profile** open on. From your first shot Fairway keeps your stats, your
round history and a sim handicap. Change the name with the pencil beside it.

**Every golfer is a profile.** In the group setup -- for a round, an endless
run or the driving range -- each row picks someone from the list, or **New
player...** to type a name; a new player becomes a profile when the group is
started or applied. Typing the name of someone already on this device picks them
rather than making a second one, and nobody can be in a group twice. Mid-round,
the player button in the top bar opens the same list: **Add player** brings
someone in on the tee of the hole being played, a new player included, and
removing someone takes them off the card (it asks first). The holes they
already finished stay in their history.

**My profile** shows one player at a time; the names across the top switch
between everyone on this device, main first. Any player can be made the main
profile, renamed, or removed with their history (it asks twice). The main
profile cannot be removed until someone else is main.

**Back up & move**, at the foot of My profile, saves every player to a file and
restores them from one -- the way to keep a history when the browser is cleared,
or to take it to another computer, the server's link or a phone. Restoring adds
to the players already there and never removes one: a player who is already on
this device keeps whichever copy was played more recently, and a different
player with a name that is taken comes in as "Sam (2)". On a first visit, the
welcome has **Restore your players from a backup file** instead of typing a name.

**It lives in this browser, on this device.** It survives reloads, closing the
browser and updating Fairway, and is gone if the browser's site data is cleared.
A private window never keeps it. The copy opened from disk and the copy on the
website are separate places to a browser, so each has its own profiles.

**Whose shots count.** Everyone's. Each ball is counted for the golfer who hit
it, and each finished hole goes on that golfer's own history. Apart from being
player 1, the main profile is recorded exactly like everyone else. The driving
range counts only its own ball total and each golfer's fastest ball speed.

**Overall** totals every ball you have hit on a course: shots, sand shots,
bunkers found, water, out of bounds, trees and houses hit, chip-ins, lip-outs,
mulligans, how far your balls have travelled and how long they have been in the
air. Your longest drive (a driver off the tee that stayed in play), longest putt
holed and fastest ball speed are kept as bests. A mulligan still counts as a
ball hit, and as a mulligan.

**Scoring** counts every finished hole by name -- holes in one, eagles, birdies,
pars and the rest -- with your average on par 3s, 4s and 5s, putts per hole,
fairways hit and greens in regulation. Decimal putting scores are named by the
nearest whole stroke. A scramble card is the team's score, so scramble rounds are
listed but kept out of these totals.

**Round history** lists every round you have finished a hole on, newest first,
with its score against par for the holes played. **Replay this course** builds
the same course again for a fresh round with whoever is in your group now; an
endless run starts again from the same first hole. A round marked *Earlier
generator* was played before Fairway's course generator last changed, so the
land comes back a little different. **Remove** (it asks twice) takes a round out
of the history -- a practice nine you'd rather not count -- so its scores leave
your scoring and your sim handicap. Your totals keep the balls you hit in it.

**The sim handicap** works like a real Handicap Index under the World Handicap
System. Each finished 9 or 18 hole round of stroke or match play becomes a score
differential, every hole capped at net double bogey (par plus five until you have
an index). Your index is the average of your best eight of the last twenty, with
the official allowance while you have fewer. It needs three rounds to start.
Fairway's courses have never been rated, so each one is rated from its length
alone; hazards, slopes and green speed do not make a course harder here the way
they would on a real card. Endless runs, three-hole rounds and unfinished rounds
do not count toward it.

## Sound

**The Swing Meter has its own sounds**: a soft rush of air that rises with the marker and falls as it comes back, a click when you set the distance, a buzz as it goes into overswing, a bright marimba run for a perfect strike, a wood block for one in the yellow zone, a thunk for a miss and a falling whistle if the swing runs out. Never with a launch monitor. Everything you hear is made as you play, never a recording, so nothing repeats. Each club has its own strike, and the ball sounds different on every surface: a soft thup on a green, a pfff into sand, a splash into water. It knocks off a trunk, rustles through leaves, clacks off rock, rattles the flagstick and thwacks off a house, and drops into the cup with a plastic knock and a rattle that settles to a skitter. Finishing a hole plays a short jingle for the score in the style of an old game console: a bouncing run and a drum for a birdie or better, two notes for par, a drooping pair for a bogey, a wobbling slide down for worse. Around you is the course itself: wind that gusts with the course's wind, birds one at a time, crickets on a warm night, water when your ball is near it -- a bubbly lap at a pond or lake, a rush at a river, a babble at a creek, surf at the sea -- the odd plane passing high overhead. **With a launch monitor armed there is no club sound**, because you hear your own strike; everything else plays. Sounds near the camera are louder than far ones, and they sit left or right as you see them. The **Sound** window, in the main menu and in the menu during a round, turns it all off or sets three volumes: everything, shots and landings, and the course around you. A browser stays silent until you first click, tap or press a key.

## Homes, waterways and presentation

Enable **Line fairways with houses** in Course studio and adjust **House occurrence** and **House setback from semi-rough**. Houses face the playing corridor, use regional colors, and occupy dry, gentle rough away from greens and tee pads, never between a tee and where its shot lands. Trees stay clear of their footprints. Houses are solid: a ball rebounds off walls and roofs and stays in play, and one resting against a wall can still be played away from it. Turn on **Houses play as out of bounds** in Course studio if you would rather reaching one cost a penalty stroke and a replay from your previous lie; it is off unless you choose it. Houses vary their roof form, porches, garages, fences and planting, sit on foundations cut to their own ground, and are outlined on the full-course map. Constrained sites may produce fewer houses.

**Rivers** (0–2) and **Creeks** (0–3) add meandering channels that can cross fairways or run between holes. Adjust their independent widths, channel depth and meandering. Creek depth is 60% of the chosen river depth. Each channel draws its own bearing and meander wavelengths, so rivers and creeks no longer run as parallel copies of one curve. Channels sweep through a fairway as one continuous curve rather than turning corners around whatever they avoid, and they route around bunkers; on a course too tight to route around one, the channel washes that bunker out rather than leaving water standing in sand. Protected tee, green, pond and bunker areas may reduce the number of accepted channels. Channels have carved beds, actual water lies, animated water and map lines, and a staged shoreline of wet margin and damp earth that widens with the channel and fades into the surrounding ground. Mown turf is kept close to the water; rough and open ground keep a much broader bank. Their water profile follows the terrain as a visual approximation; this is not a drainage simulation and bridges are not included yet.

The ground is levelled into a shelf beneath each pond during generation, so a pond no longer needs a naturally flat site; it still sits below its rim and outer banks, with fine shore and bed geometry. Wherever a pond, lake, river or creek meets a fairway, the mown corridor stops behind a full band of semi-rough that comes round the shoreline, starting where the damp shore soil ends so the whole width of it is visible. The band is the hole's own semi-rough width, so it widens and narrows with that setting and disappears entirely at zero. The turf reads as mown around the hazard rather than sliced off at it — and a channel crossing a fairway ends the mown corridor on one bank and restarts it on the other instead of running underneath. Ponds can sit well out in the rough, come up to the playing corridor, bite into it, or cross it completely and split the hole into two landing areas — water carried off the tee is a shape golf uses and the generator can now produce it. Splits are uncommon (about 6% of holes at the default water setting, 13% at the maximum) and the carry is a golf shot rather than a wall: 33 yards typically, 101 at the most extreme measured. Ponds, lakes, rivers and creeks are cut into the ground the way bunkers are: the water sits about a metre below its rim, the ground is held out to that rim and then drops over a couple of metres, and a lip of wet soil meets the turf. **The ocean is the exception** — a coast is not an excavation, so island and links shores get a shaped foreshore and a real beach that is painted as sand and plays as sand, wide where the shore is flat and narrow where it is steep. **Island courses carry no inland water** — no ponds, lakes, rivers or creeks, whatever those settings say. The ocean is the hazard, it is already in play on most shots, and a strip of land with sea on every side is the worst possible place to site a pond. The settings keep their values, so switching a course to island and back returns the water it had. On Links, which also has a coast, water is kept on land: a channel is trimmed at the shoreline and fades out where it meets the sea rather than being drawn along the ocean floor, and a channel is chosen for how much of the course it crosses rather than for how long it is. They share the same wet-margin and damp-earth shoreline as rivers and creeks rather than a sand ring. At high **Water frequency** settings large ponds crowd each other, so about one pond per hole is placed however many are requested. Green surroundings blend over a much broader area to soften steep shoulders. Green difficulty continues to control the putting surface itself.

The number tiles and the shot line stay on screen through the flight, counting carry and total up as the ball goes. A shot holds on the final lie for **three seconds**, or four and a half while the camera shows the whole shot, before play moves on. A replay holds the final lie for **three seconds** before returning. Taking a putt animates the flag upward out of the cup. Hole completion leaves the cup visible for **three seconds** before opening the scorecard; its existing eight-second next-hole countdown starts afterward. Explicit skip/continue controls remain available.

## Reporting a problem

**Help & controls → Report a problem → Copy diagnostic.** It puts a short
summary on your clipboard and shows it in the box underneath, so you can read
what you are about to send before you send it.

It carries the build you are running, the generator and settings versions, the
mode and hole you were on, your browser, your graphics card, the screen and
the frame rate, and the last few errors if anything went wrong. When you are
playing a saved-shaped course it also carries the course code, which is enough
to rebuild the exact landscape you were looking at.

**Nothing is sent anywhere.** Fairway makes no network requests at all, and
this is no exception -- the text goes to your clipboard and stops there. What
happens to it afterwards is your decision.

The frame rate is reported as a median and a worst frame rather than an
average, because an average hides the thing worth reporting: a run that mostly
holds 60 and stalls twice a second averages out respectable and is horrible to
play.

## Launch monitor setup

A browser page cannot listen for a launch monitor itself, so the download carries a small program that does: **run_fairway_server**. It serves the game to this computer and to phones and tablets on your Wi-Fi, and it receives shots in the **GSPro Open Connect (v1)** format that launch-monitor connectors already send to GSPro. Nothing needs installing; the download's README walks through starting it on Windows, macOS and Linux.

1. **Start run_fairway_server.** A window opens with two links: one for this computer (`http://127.0.0.1:1922`) and one for a phone or tablet on the same Wi-Fi. Keep the window open while you play.
2. **Connect your launch monitor through its connector.** With **rēlā** (Windows, [docs.rela.golf](https://docs.rela.golf/)): pick your monitor under **Device** and click **Search**, set **Simulator** to **GSPro**, and in rēlā's **Settings** set the simulator address to **127.0.0.1** and the port to **1921**. Any other Open Connect connector works the same way: point it at the computer running Fairway, TCP port 1921.
3. **Open the game** from one of the links, then **Launch monitor** on the main menu (or in Tools during a round) and **Connect bridge**. The address is filled in for you, and whichever address last connected is remembered on that device. Tick **Arm monitor for live shots**.
4. **Select the club and aim in Fairway, and hit.** Shots are played from measured speed, launch angles and spin. The Swing Meter and its curve, height and spin play no part. **The lie does**: from semi-rough, rough or sand your measured ball speed loses the same share a Swing Meter shot loses there (4%, 10% and 20%). Everything else -- launch, direction, spin and axis -- flies as measured. The ball speed tile shows the speed your monitor measured, with what the lie took off beside it in red, and carry and total say *rough* or *sand* in red because they already include it. Switch it off for the round in **Format & tees** (*Lie penalty for launch-monitor shots*) and monitor shots fly exactly as measured from everywhere.

The panel also validates and plays sample JSON independently of hardware. This sample is a real physics input, not evidence of a connected device. Bridge connectivity and device connectivity are reported separately. Incoming shots are rejected while unarmed, during flight, or while a hole/lie selection needs attention. There is no silent shot queue. The bridge sends the monitor a success only after the browser accepts the shot. Club/handedness messages use code 201; the community ready extension uses 202. Duplicate device/shot-number pairs are rejected within each browser connection.

Two markers float in the world rather than in a bar: the **flag** shows yards to the pin — with what the shot **plays like** under it — and the **aim point** shows how far away the spot you are aiming at is. **They take turns**, because side by side they often sat on top of each other: the flag's is the one on screen, and while you move the aim it fades out and the aim point's fades in, swapping back about a second after you stop. The aim marker also steps aside at rest when the spot is within a few yards of the flag (never while you are aiming, when the flag's label has faded anyway). On the green it shows too, for everyone: with no putt preview, the aim point is how far you mean to hit the putt.

**Plays like** is the distance the shot to the hole would be on level ground in still air at sea level: "333 yards to hole, plays 322" means hit it as if it were 322. It is worked out by the game's own ball model, not a rule of thumb: the club in your hands, at a full swing, is flown once in today's wind, altitude and temperature to the hole's height, and once in still air on the level, and the hole's distance is scaled by how much farther or shorter the first one gets there. So it changes with the club you pick, uses your ball-flight settings, and agrees with how the ball will actually fly. Uphill, into the wind, in cold air it plays longer; downhill, downwind, up a mountain it plays shorter. It is not shown for a putt.

**A group can mix the two ways of playing.** Once a launch monitor has connected on this device, the group editor asks two more things of each golfer: **plays with** -- *Launch monitor* or *Swing Meter* -- and which **hand** they swing with. Leave the monitor armed for the whole round. On a launch-monitor golfer's turn everything is as below; on a Swing Meter golfer's turn the Swing Meter comes up, the monitor window shrinks to its strip (the light stays, and it says who is up and that the monitor is waiting), and a ball hit into the monitor is refused, as it would be unarmed. Change how a golfer plays at any time from the player button -- it applies from their next shot -- and both choices are remembered with the golfer for the next round. The hand is what the monitor is told on that golfer's turn; the Swing Meter has no hand. If the monitor disconnects on a monitor golfer's turn the game says so and the Swing Meter comes up, so the group is never stuck waiting. A golfer set to *Launch monitor* -- everyone, until you choose otherwise -- plays off the monitor whenever it is armed, exactly as before groups could mix.

**With a monitor armed, a launch monitor window takes the place of the shot controls.** It holds only what you need at the mat: the ball light — **Ready** in green when a ball is on the mat, **Finding ball** in amber while the monitor hunts for one, flashing, **No monitor** in red, flashing faster, when the bridge is running but no device is talking to it — in words as well as colour, with the whole window outlined to match; the **Armed** switch; the **shot line**, with Skip; the club, which is sent to your monitor — any club your bag holds; and **Reconnect**. The lie is shown under the light: *Rough · −10% ball speed*, or *no penalty (switched off)* when the round has it off. The Swing Meter, the curve, height and spin, the tee and the shot button are gone, because ball speed, launch, direction, spin and axis all come off the device, and so are the aim arrows and the aim distance box. **Aim with the keyboard, the mouse or the map**: click the course or the map to aim, ← → turn the line, and **↑ ↓ move the aim point further and shorter** — slowly at first and faster the longer you hold, a foot at a time inside twenty yards. That works for every club, the putter included: a putt starts aimed at the hole, and you can aim it anywhere. Turn **Armed** off and the shot controls come back; with a monitor connected but not armed they carry a slim strip at the top with the same light and switch, which is how you pick the monitor up again. The window moves and resizes like any panel. Club numbers are dashes when your device sends no club data. **Playing by hand and playing off a monitor each keep their own Shot data** — which tiles, and in what order — and the tiles keep their own places for each, so moving one while armed leaves it where it was for playing by hand. (A phone uses its own three either way.) This applies in every mode that shows the tiles, the driving range included.

If a connector will not talk, start the server with `FAIRWAY_LOG=debug` set for per-message logging: every raw payload in, every reply code out, and each shot decoded into mph and rpm, which is where wrong units and swapped spin fields actually show up. Ports are set with `FAIRWAY_TCP_PORT` (the launch monitor's, 1921) and `FAIRWAY_HTTP_PORT` (the browser's, 1922): if a connector is fixed to GSPro's standard port 921, set `FAIRWAY_TCP_PORT=921` (Windows allows it; macOS and Linux may need permission for ports under 1024). The server accepts browsers from this computer and from home-network addresses only, and one browser controls it at a time. If a connector runs on another machine (for example, a Raspberry Pi), set `FAIRWAY_TCP_HOST` to this computer's home-network address and allow that port on your trusted local network. The Open Connect protocol has no authentication or encryption, and the server has no password: keep it off networks you do not trust and never expose it to the internet.

Potential connector paths include PiTrac, the Garmin R10 community connector, Rapsodo MLM2PRO's connector, and OpenSkyPlus. These are protocol-level integration paths, **not a verified hardware compatibility list**. Device firmware, vendor licenses, connector versions, and operating systems affect support. This project does not include vendor drivers. The project's research notes (RESEARCH.md, in the repository) list the verified project links and protocol assumptions.
