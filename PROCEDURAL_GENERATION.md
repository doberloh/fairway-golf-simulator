# How Fairway generates a course

Fully procedural generation is a good fit for this simulator. It needs constraints to create playable golf, but it does not need a catalogue of prebuilt holes. The same seed and settings reproduce the same course within this generator version. Changing the generator can change what an older seed produces.

## What is generated

1. **Plan the round.** Requested yardage determines total par within 68–72 for eighteen holes or 34–36 for nine. A seeded choice selects a valid mix of par 3s, 4s and 5s, then shuffles their order. Independently varied hole distances are scaled to the requested total. Beyond the par bounds, lengths continue scaling. Very short or long requests can therefore fall outside conventional par-specific yardage ranges, as requested.
2. **Build playing lines.** Each hole gets a continuous centerline, with seeded curvature, turn size, direction and pivot location. Par 3s are always straight; dogleg frequency still allows deliberately straight par 4/5 holes. Selected doglegs balance left and right across a course; the selected turning point is a guideline with earlier and later variation.
3. **Generate fairway edges.** Each side receives its own 4–8 control points, random locations and widths, with smooth interpolation. A shared broad taper and independent local changes produce expanding, contracting and multiple landing areas without selecting named outline templates. Width limits keep the fairway playable. Par 3s can connect the tee area to the fairway.
4. **Shape the putting surface, then cut the hole.** A green is four things a green architect actually builds: a broad TILT (mostly drainage, ordinarily two or three per cent), crossing RIDGES that break it into sections, a DISH that is a punchbowl if it gathers and a turtleback if it sheds, and a TIER whose face is the steepest ground on the surface. The ridges are rounded square waves rather than sines, because a green is shelves with faces between them — a pure sine green is uniformly sloped everywhere and has nowhere to cut a hole. The slider is superlinear, so the bottom and middle of the range sit where they always did and the change is spent at the top: at the default of 35 a green averages 1.2% slope with 1.3 ft of relief, and at 100 it averages 5.3%, reaches 18.5% on a tier face, carries 5.6 ft of relief, and still keeps 14% of its surface under 2.5% so there is somewhere to cut a hole. The contour is anchored at the green's **centre**, not at the cup, because the cup moves and the ground does not.

   The cup is then chosen from that contour by `choosePin`, scored on the slope under it and the green left around it. `pinDay` — Thursday through Sunday — sets a target slope and a hard cap; difficulty is slope, while distance to the edge is a safety floor of 3 m rather than a second dial. Bands rotate front, middle, back by hole number starting at the front. Pin choice draws from its own seeded stream so it cannot shift anything else on the course, and nothing else on the course is placed relative to it.
5. **Sculpt bunkers and hazards.** Seeded sizes, proportions and continuous boundary waves generate green and bunker outlines. Water uses sampled curving banks with changing widths, shaped relative to the nearby fairway edge. A clearance buffer protects turf; identical bank profiles drive the rendered water, terrain basin and map. Bunker count, position, distance from the fringe and placement inside fairways follow the studio settings. This is a constrained mathematical shape generator, not an unrestricted landscape-design AI.
6. **Place three tee sets.** Blue sets the reference route length. White and red move forward by independently varied amounts and shift slightly to either side of the playing line. All sets are always built; the golfer selects a tee during play or round setup.
7. **Route the whole course.** The chosen footprint provides a seed-varied guiding curve: an organic sweep, elongated loop, crescent, winding ribbon, original square, figure eight, butterfly, clover, spiral, horseshoe, rounded triangle, diamond, coastal sweep or island chain. The dropdown includes small selectable SVG previews. These guide overall land use rather than prescribing individual hole shapes. Seeded candidate positions and bearings place holes near the preceding green. Corridor clearance checks reject overlaps; distance from the guiding curve, progression, compactness and walking distance guide the choice. A fallback expands the footprint if a tight placement fails. This remains a heuristic, not a certified golf-course safety or architecture model.
8. **Build one landscape.** Regional landform rules create ridges, dunes, channels and biome vegetation around the routed holes. One connected mesh carries all playing surfaces. Extra detail around greens, bunkers, pond beds/shorelines and flowing channels joins the wider terrain without cracks. The distant landscape shares the detailed ground’s perimeter vertices, eliminating exposed rectangular tile edges. Trees grow around the routed corridors, giving the course an irregular outer woodland boundary. Ball contact, shading, hazard boundaries and maps consume the same course data.

## Why keep rules?

Unconstrained randomness readily creates impassable fairways, overlapping holes, flooded tees and unplayable greens. Rules constrain those outcomes while the seed continuously varies the geometry. The remaining mathematical choices—smooth curves, bounded widths, basin excavation and terrain functions—are reusable building blocks, not a finite list of complete holes.

The current approach creates much more variety than selecting a few named shapes, but it cannot guarantee that every random course has the strategic quality of a human-designed course. Further improvement should evaluate generated routes for shot choices, forced carries, recovery areas and walking distances, rather than reintroducing a fixed hole catalogue.

## Maps and green reading

Hole maps now fit the complete generated fairway, hazards, green, tees and current ball, including wide doglegs. Drawing and click-to-aim share an invertible projection with the same left/right orientation as the playing view. Replay maps follow the recorded shot’s hole.

The three green-reading layers are switched from three icon buttons in the map header, separately or together:

- A 1.5 m grid colored by actual gradient: blue under 1%, green 1–2%, yellow 2–3%, orange 3–5%, red above 5%.
- Animated markers starting at grid vertices, following the changing downhill gradient and moving faster on steeper ground.
- A relative-elevation heat map, blue low to red high, with contour lines every 10 cm. Its triangles match the terrain and leave the cup opening clear.

These tools reveal the same ground heights used for putting. The heat map shows relative elevation; grid colors show slope steepness.

Once the ball is on the putting surface the map reframes on the green alone and paints that same contour field from directly above, contours included. Its bounds are sampled from the green outline rather than assumed circular, because greens are stretched by `greenAspect` and wobble with the hole seed. Its colour range is taken from the putting surface alone: measured against the surrounding ground, a green in a hollow scales against the bank behind it and renders as one flat colour. The tile is cached per green, and nothing is drawn when the green has no relief at all — which is the case on the driving range, where green reading is disabled outright.

## Water fitting, green surrounds and housing

Green-to-surround transitions extend 40–100 metres according to the height difference, rather than blending abruptly into distant ridges. The modifier bounds include the full transition support. This softens shoulders without changing the green difficulty control.

The ground is settled to a level shelf under each pond rather than hunting for a site that happens to be flat. Each pond reads a target elevation off the base landform around its rim and eases the surrounding ground toward it; green and tee shaping is applied afterwards and still wins, so a pond can never flatten a green complex. The shelf margin scales with the pond, so shrinking a pond to fit steep ground actually reduces the footprint it is judged over. Tee pads read their height from that same shaped field rather than the raw landform, and ramp off it over a distance that grows with the drop, so a tee on a hillside becomes a terrace instead of a pad with a wall around it. Each pad carries a mown collar about 1.7 times its own size, classified as semi-rough and claimed only where the ground is otherwise rough, so a fairway, green or bunker beside a tee keeps its own surface. Pad and collar are sized from one pair of exported constants that both the lie classifier and the ground shader read, so the painted surface and the played lie cannot drift apart. Rim elevations on the finished terrain typically vary by a few centimetres. The remaining rim sampling, shrinking and removal stay as a safety net for sites that other features distort. The flat water level sits below the lowest sampled rim and outer-bank terrain rather than following the height at the pond centre, and the selected minimum/maximum depth still measures from the water surface to the excavated floor.

Ponds are generated against the fairway edge with a gap that may be negative, so water can sit well out in the rough or come right up to the playing corridor, but never inside it. Because a pond's banks are anchored outside the fairway edge, enlarging one moves it away from play rather than into it.

River and creek controls request a bounded number of course-wide meandering corridors. Tees, greens and fitted ponds have protected clearance areas; accepted paths are rounded, given variable widths and terrain-fitted levels, and indexed spatially. That same path drives bed carving, water surfaces, water lies and map lines. Par threes are not given a fairway at all: the ball is played through the air to the green, so a full corridor down the hole is turf nobody uses and it swallows the tee complex. They get an approach instead, beginning at `mowStart` rather than `fairwayStart` — never wider than the green it fronts and never covering more than the last 45% of the hole — and every bunker on a par three is greenside, because there is no fairway to bunker. `fairwayStart` still anchors bunker and pond placement on every hole, so par threes keep their hazards. Rivers may cross fairways. Channels are a downhill-graded approximation, not a hydraulic network; unsuitable routes can be omitted. Creek depths are 60% of river depth, and no bridges are generated yet. A channel runs only as far as it can stay within its cut budget, and fades out where it is trimmed.

Optional homes are sampled along both sides of the fairways with occurrence and setback controls. Sites must be dry rough, gently sloping, spaced from other homes and clear of tee/green complexes. Houses face their corridor and trees are excluded from their footprints. They currently are decorative scenery without collision rules.

## Large lakes and downhill streams

Large lakes are additional continuous procedural bank profiles, requested with count and typical-diameter sliders. Candidate search favors low, gentler open ground outside the playing corridors. They share the pond basin/reflection/map pipeline. A lake that extends across nearest-hole tiles retains its own surface and GPU ownership; the per-hole atlas limit remains four ponds/lakes. Available space may reduce the requested count.

Each channel draws its own bearing, meander wavelengths, amplitudes and phases, so a river and a creek on the same course are independent curves rather than offset copies of one. Obstacles — tees, greens, fitted ponds and bunkers — are passed on a side chosen once from the undisturbed line, using a smoothed displacement rather than a hard clamp, so a detour is one long curve instead of a corner at each influence boundary. Corner cutting and a curvature limit then hold every bend wider than the channel itself, which is what keeps the bank from folding through itself where a channel crosses a fairway. Bunker avoidance is attempted at a generous berth and then a tight one; where a course leaves no route at all, the channel washes the crossed bunker out rather than leaving water standing in sand, and bunker count is already an upper target.

River candidates are evaluated in both directions and the lower-excavation profile is selected. Water elevations stay below sampled terrain and descend continuously, with longitudinal grades limited to 0.015–2.5%. Because levels only descend, one hollow near the head pins every station after it, and any later ridge would have to be trenched through to hold that level; rather than cut a gorge, the profile keeps the longest run whose bed stays within `MAX_CUT` of the ground beside it and trims the rest. A trimmed end is a headwater or a sink, so the channel closes down to nothing over `FADE` instead of ending at a blunt face, and a candidate whose surviving run is too short is rejected outright — on very hilly courses that can mean fewer channels than requested. Graded shoulders carve the remaining channel through intervening ground while protecting tees, greens and pond banks. This produces consistent downstream levels without claiming catchment hydrology or connected tributaries. Banks step through a wet margin and damp earth and then fade into the surrounding cut, each band scaled to the channel width and to the surface being crossed: mown turf is maintained close to the water, while unmaintained ground keeps a margin about three times wider. The change between the two is gradual, beginning roughly 70 m before a river reaches a fairway, so the bank tapers rather than stepping. Across the crossing itself the turf boundaries blend and the mowing stripes fade, which is the one place the course does not draw a crisp edge; it affects appearance only, and a ball's lie is still decided at the true boundary. The bank stays earth-toned throughout, and grass is kept off it, so a channel does not trace a coloured ribbon across the ground it crosses. River/creek surfaces share the pond reflector, approximated at the nearest station elevation, and thin out at the waterline.
