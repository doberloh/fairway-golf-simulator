Fairway Sound Lab

Built on branch sounds. Nothing here is in the game yet.

Every sound on this page is made live by the browser. There are no recordings, so there is nothing to license or attribute, and the game file barely grows. Each play is new: strikes vary a little every time, and the background sounds are random processes (gusts, birds, bubbles, passing planes) rather than a loop. Leave a scene running for ten minutes and nothing will come round again.

Use headphones or decent speakers; a laptop speaker loses the low thumps. For each sound, pick the take (A, B or C) that is closest, nudge the sliders under Adjust, and type a note. Copy my notes (bottom right) gathers every change and note into one block to paste back to me. Your settings stay in this browser between visits.

Round 4: every sound is signed off and playing in the game. Each sound has one take, exactly as you set it: the marimba for every score, water A, the flagstick A, brick for every house.     6. Also done this round, in the game

    The flagstick is solid while it is in the hole. A ball that hits it bounces off, the same way a ball bounces off a tree. It is out of the way whenever the game pulls it: on the green, and whenever the putter is in hand.

    Houses were solid already, but in the wrong place. On any hole that does not run due north, each house's solid box was turned by that hole's angle, and it stood 2.6 m above every roof. On a test course of 57 houses, a third of each house's footprint disagreed with the drawing. Now it matches, and balls skimming past a wall or over a roof go by.

    Flagsticks stay visible from a distance. Past the point where the stick would shrink to a few pixels, the stick and its flag grow on screen so they keep a findable size, and the pole is never thinner than about two pixels. Close up they are exactly to size, and the ball always hits the real-sized stick.

    8. How each sound is made

    SoundMade fromWhere the numbers come from
    DriverRinging tones (the head's vibrations), a short crack, the ball's own tick and a low thumpPublished: a titanium driver strike centres near 4-4.5 kHz and lasts under 0.2 s; the ball rings near 1.5 kHz. The exact set of tones is set by ear.
    SwingDarker noise through a narrow band whose pitch rises into the strike and falls afterSet by ear (round 2: round 1's wide band sounded like hiss).
    Irons, wedgesA hard, very short crack and the ball's own sound, then the divot (thump, tearing grass, clods); no metal ringFrom your round-1 note; set by ear.
    Tree trunkA hard crack and short, bright wooden tones, like a pine boardFrom your round-1 note; set by ear.
    Flagstick, houseThe stick: a dull fibreglass knock, the metal fitting rattling in the cup, the flag snapping. A house: four materials (siding, brick, a roof with the ball rattling down, a window tink)Set by ear.
    Score stingsShort phrases on a synthesised marimba, bells or electric piano: better scores climb and brighten, par settles, bogeys fall softlySet by ear. No crowd, by your call.
    CupA dull plastic "tok", then a rattle of shrinking bounces, coloured by the hollow tubeHole 4.25 in across and at least 4 in deep, with a moulded plastic liner (USGA, liner patents). The ball drops about 0.1 m. The tube's hollow note is near 640 Hz, worked out from its depth.
    PutterShort tones split into putter (below 2 kHz) and ball (2-4 kHz)Published: Barrass, Roberts and Jones (2006).
    Turf landingsA falling-pitch thump, the ball's tick, filtered noise for grassSet by ear.
    WaterSlap, spray, a rising "plunk", then dozens of bubblesBubble pitch from bubble size (Minnaert); the rising pitch from van den Doel (2005).
    WindFiltered noise moved by a random gust processSet by ear; follows the game's wind speed.
    BirdsWhistled tones that follow pitch contours; harsh calls through voice-like filters. One bird at a timeSongbirds between 1 and 8 kHz (Cornell's All About Birds).
    Dove (round 2)"cooOOoo-wooo-woo-woooo": one slurred opening that climbs to the high note and falls back, then three steady low coos, the last the longest. Nearly pure tone, soft onsets, a breath of airOne measured song: 390 Hz with the high note at 650 Hz. Doves coo with the bill shut through an inflated throat, which filters out the harmonics (Fletcher et al., ring dove). The timings are set by ear.
    Crickets (round 2)A chorus of tree crickets chirping together: 8 pulses per chirp in groups of 2, 3 and 3, the last of each group longer. On warm nights (18 C and up), a field cricket or two on top: 3-pulse chirps near 4.5-5 kHz, each on its own clockChirp rate from temperature (Dolbear's law); pitch rises with temperature, about 3.0 kHz at 15 C to 4.6 kHz at 32 C. Snowy tree crickets synchronise with their neighbours (Walker, 1969). Silent below 13 C.
    PlanesRumble (jet) or engine buzz (propeller), moved along a real flight pathDistance, travel time, Doppler and the ground reflection that makes a passing jet "whoosh".
